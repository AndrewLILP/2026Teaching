Lesson 1 of 25 · 55 minutes

Data or information?

Every app and game you have built quietly produces data. This term you'll learn to turn that data into information that helps someone make a decision. We'll use the same five projects all term: two coin-collecting games, a text adventure, a fitness app and a canteen ordering app.

Identifying & defining

Teaching advice

Timing: hook 4 min · vocabulary 5 min · five rows 12 min · data to decision 8 min · sort 6 min · DART 6 min · tasks 7 min · checkpoint and exam question 5 min · reflection 2 min.

Discussion focus: this is a talking lesson. The five-rows activity works best in pairs, then whole-class: let students argue about what each value means before you open the column headings.

Learning intentions

By the end of this lesson I can:

  • explain the difference between data and information
  • describe how processing turns data into information that supports a decision
  • describe the purposes of analysing data, using examples from games and apps.
Success criteria: I can sort statements into data and information, and for two of the five projects I can trace one example from data to information to decision.

What does 42 tell you?

The canteen manager opens the Canteen Click ordering app at the end of the week. The screen shows one line:

Canteen Click · this week Sausage roll: 42

She has to decide how many sausage rolls to order for next week. Talk with the person next to you: is "42" enough to make that decision? What else would you want to know?

  • 42 out of how many orders? Is that a lot or a little?
  • Were they spread across the week, or did most sell on one day?
  • Did 42 different students buy one each, or did someone buy a big batch at once?
  • How does 42 compare with other items, and with last week?

Every question asks for the number to be compared, grouped or put in context. That's what analysing data does. Later this term you'll find something surprising hiding inside that 42.

From data to decision

One record is data: facts with no meaning yet. When we process many records, by counting, grouping, averaging or comparing them, and put the result in context, we get information. Information is only worth having if it helps someone decide something.

Watch the numbers

"42.6%" is still a number, but it isn't raw data. It was calculated from 54 records and it's about something (Level 3). Processing plus context is what makes it information.

Why analyse data?

People analyse data for a handful of reasons. Most real analyses do more than one of these.

Purposes of analysing data with an example for each
PurposeThe question it answersExample
DescribeWhat happened?How many canteen orders were placed each day?
ExplainWhy did it happen?Why do Cave of Echoes players quit?
PredictWhat is likely to happen next?How many burgers will sell next Friday?
Decide or improveWhat should we change?Which jump in Pixel Pockets should be made easier?
MonitorIs it still working?Did StepUp's week-3 challenge stop the drop in workouts?

Read like an analyst

The text below is from the producer of Pixel Pockets, writing to her team after a round of playtesting. It mixes data, information and decisions, the way real reports do.

DART · text marking · 6 min

Copy or print the text. Mark each idea with one colour:

Data: raw facts Information: processed, with meaning Decision: what we'll do

Our Godot build logged 127 attempts from 35 playtesters. Each record stores the level, coins, jumps, time, outcome and the x position where the attempt ended. One attempt ended at x = 1491 after 32.8 seconds. When we grouped every failed Level 2 attempt by position, 16 of the 42 failures ended between x = 1400 and 1599, and nearly all of those were falls. So the gap jump, not the enemies, is what stops players on Level 2.

We will widen the landing platform after the gap by 64 pixels. Then we'll run another round of playtests with the same logging, so we can check whether the change worked.

Data: 127 attempts were logged · each record stores level, coins, jumps, time, outcome and x position · one attempt ended at x = 1491 after 32.8 seconds.

Information: 16 of the 42 Level 2 failures ended between x = 1400 and 1599, mostly falls · the gap jump, not the enemies, is what stops players.

Decision: widen the landing platform by 64 pixels · run another round of playtests to check the change worked.

Up for debate: is "127 attempts from 35 playtesters" data or information? It's a count, so some processing has happened, but it only describes the dataset's size, not a pattern. Most analysts would call it a description of the data. What matters is that you can justify your choice.

Exam-style question

Short answer 3 marks

Pixel Pockets is a 2D platform game. Every time a player attempts a level, the game records the level number, coins collected, number of jumps, time taken, outcome and the x position where the attempt ended.

Question: Distinguish between data and information. Use an example of each from Pixel Pockets in your answer.

Write your answer in your notebook before opening the marking guide.

Marking guide
CriteriaMarks
Clearly distinguishes data from information (processing and/or context) AND gives a correct Pixel Pockets example of each3
Defines both terms correctly with one correct example, OR gives correct examples of both with a weak distinction2
Provides some relevant information about data or information1

Sample answer: Data is raw facts with no meaning on their own, such as one attempt's outcome Fell and end position 1491. Information is data that has been processed and given context so it means something to a person. For example, grouping all failed Level 2 attempts by position shows that most falls happen at the same gap, which tells the developer which jump to fix.

Common error: giving a number as the example of information just because it's a number (for example, "32.8 seconds"). A single time from one attempt is still data.
