Teacher notes · not for students

Good News? Data: demo double lesson

Two 55-minute lessons for Stage 5 Enterprise information systems: Analysing data. Lesson 1 is the observed demo and follows a strict I do → We do → You do sequence. In Lesson 2 students create their own chart. The lesson page is also the slide deck: press P to present.

LessonBig ideaStudents produce
1 · Is the world really ending?Data becomes information when it is processed and given context. How it is collected and displayed decides whether it can be trusted.Anonymous survey data, a written evaluation of a misleading chart, an exit ticket
2 · Trends, outliers and your own chartVerify and validate before concluding. A chart’s form should match the job it has to do.A hand-drawn chart with an information headline, two peer feedback slips, one revision

Before the lesson

  • Print: one student booklet per student (8 A4 pages; double-sided is fine). Print one survey slip per student plus spares (4 per page; cut them up beforehand).
  • Gather: one large envelope, mini whiteboards and markers, and coloured pens for the Lesson 2 revisions.
  • Test the page on the teaching computer: open Lesson 1, press P, and step through to “Count the class”. Click Load sample data to check the charts draw, then click Clear.
  • Same computer for both lessons. The class counts are saved in that browser, so Lesson 2 opens with them already loaded. On a different computer, re-type them from your photo of the board.
  • Queue the video and check both clip start times. YouTube can show ads. If your school has ClickView or another approved way to download, use it so there is no ad and you can stop the clip exactly on time.
  • Lesson 2 seating: arrange desks so booklets can pass two places clockwise without confusion.
  • Keep the slips. Collect them back into the envelope at the end of the count in Lesson 1. Lesson 2 needs question F.

If the tech fails

Run the count on the board using the tally grid in the whiteboard plan, and draw the stacked bar chart by hand. For the “Evaluate the display” slide, sketch charts B and C on the board from the class counts. The printed tobacco chart on booklet page 4 still works for the You do task.

Learning intentions and success criteria

Write these on the board before students arrive (left panel of the whiteboard plan).

Lesson 1 · We are learning to

  • tell the difference between data and information
  • collect data in a way that protects people’s privacy
  • judge whether a data display informs or misleads

Lesson 1 · I can

  • explain the difference between data and information using an example from today’s survey
  • identify PII and explain how our survey protected it
  • trace our survey data through input, storage, transmission, processing and output
  • use the visualisation checklist to explain whether a chart shows data fairly

Lesson 2 · We are learning to

  • verify and validate data before drawing conclusions from it
  • use a visualisation to reveal a trend or an outlier
  • improve a design using peer feedback

Lesson 2 · I can

  • find and correct an error in a dataset by checking it against the source
  • explain why an outlier should be checked before it is removed
  • design a chart that meets the visualisation checklist and shows a trend or an outlier
  • give specific feedback using the checklist, and make one improvement from the feedback I receive

Answers

Lesson 1: sort activity

Data: the single tick, 93.5, the column of hours. Information: “Most of our class…”, the 1990–2024 sentence, “The typical student…”.

Lesson 1: quick check

Q1 D handwriting · Q2 B “Most of us think smoking is falling” · Q3 A small differences look bigger · Q4 C a bar per topic, split into answers.

Lesson 1: the four displays (sample data, n = 28)

  • A. Stacked bars: fair. Every item on the checklist is met.
  • B. Only the bad news: breaks 5 (cherry-picked: “Worse” only) and 1 (shouting headline). Links directly to the framing effect in clip 1.
  • C. Better vs worse: breaks 3. With the sample data (5 vs 15), the axis starts at 4, so the bars are drawn 1 and 11 units tall. The Worse bar looks 11 times bigger, but the real ratio is 3 times. The page calculates this from the class’s own numbers.
  • D. Line across topics: breaks 4. Topics A–E have no order, so a line implies a trend that doesn’t exist.

Lesson 1: booklet task, “Smoking has COLLAPSED!”

Real figures (WHO): adult tobacco use 26.2% in 2010 and 19.5% in 2024. The booklet chart’s axis starts at 18%.

  • Checklist: 1 ✗ (exaggerated headline; it also says “smoking” when the data is all tobacco use) · 2 ✗ (no source, no units on the axis) · 3 ✗ (axis starts at 18) · 4 ✓ (bars can compare two years; a line is also acceptable with a reason) · 5 ✗ (only two years are shown; the data goes back to 2000) · 6 ✓ (values are labelled) · 7 ✗ (doesn’t say who: adults aged 15+, worldwide).
  • Foundation sentence: “The data shows tobacco use fell from 26.2% to 19.5%, but the chart makes it look like it almost disappeared.”
  • Core headline example: “Adult tobacco use worldwide fell from about 1 in 4 to about 1 in 5 (2010–2024).”
  • Extension: real relative fall = (26.2 − 19.5) ÷ 26.2 = 25.6% (6.7 percentage points). Bar heights above the 18% baseline: 8.2 and 1.5, so the visual fall = (8.2 − 1.5) ÷ 8.2 = 81.7%. Someone campaigning to show anti-smoking laws worked might want the dramatic version; publishing it is misleading even though every number is true.

Lesson 2: Dataset A verification

The 2005 value is typed as 623; the source says 62.3. This is a transcription error (a missed decimal point). Verified series, deaths per 1,000 live births: 1990 93.5 · 1995 87.5 · 2000 76.7 · 2005 62.3 · 2010 50.6 · 2015 42.9 · 2020 39.2 · 2024 37.4.

Extension calculation: 1990–2015: (93.5 − 42.9) ÷ 25 ≈ 2.0 per year. 2015–2024: (42.9 − 37.4) ÷ 9 ≈ 0.6 per year. Progress slowed to under a third of its earlier pace. Total fall 1990–2024 is 60%.

Lesson 2: hours sample data

26 entries. Validation rejects “two” (type check) and 25 (range check), leaving 24 valid values. Quartiles are the medians of the lower and upper halves: Q1 = 2, Q3 = 3, IQR = 1, fences 0.5 and 4.5. Outliers: 6 and 18. Median 2.25, mean 3.1, mean without outliers 2.3. Discussion point: 6 hours is plausible and real, but it is still an outlier. Outlier does not mean error.

Lesson 2: quick check

Q1 C check against the source · Q2 A check it: an outlier isn’t always an error · Q3 D type check.

Likely misconceptions

Students may think…Correct it with…
Information is just “more data”.93.5 vs the full 93.5 sentence: same number, but now it has context and answers a question.
No name means anonymous.Handwriting, rare answers, small groups, and the metadata an online form collects (case study).
Starting the axis higher “zooms in” and is more accurate.Chart C: bar length shows the value, so cutting the bottom off changes the comparison.
A line chart works for any data.Chart D: reorder the topics and the “trend” changes. Lines need an ordered x-axis, like time.
Outliers are mistakes, so delete them.18 hours passed validation; 6 hours is plausible. Check first, then keep, fix or exclude with a recorded reason.
Verification and validation are the same thing.Validation applies rules at entry; verification compares data with its source. 623 passes a 0–1,000 range check but fails verification.

Differentiation and grading

Tasks are tiered Foundation (D–E), Core (C) and Extension (A–B), matching the Stage 5 course performance descriptors. Each lesson page shows students the grade strip for the two descriptor strands in play.

  • EAL/D and literacy: sentence starters on every writing task; vocabulary is hand-written with an example; the page’s font toggle includes OpenDyslexic.
  • Students with adjustments: the survey is tick-only. For the chart, Foundation students draw one topic as three bars.
  • Extension: the misleading-chart task needs a percentage-change calculation, and the trend task needs rates of change: real Stage 5 mathematics.

For observing colleagues

Lesson 1 is the demo. Useful things to watch for:

  • Gradual release: each new idea is modelled (I do), practised together with the class’s own data (We do), then applied independently to a new chart (You do). Labels on each slide show which stage it is.
  • Real data, generated in the room: the chart students evaluate is built from their own answers. The misleading versions use their own numbers too.
  • Privacy modelled, not just taught: no names, tick boxes, shuffle and swap, totals only. Students experience the protection before they name it.
  • Check-for-understanding moments: sort hinge (slide 6), mini whiteboards (slide 9), finger voting on checklist items (slide 11), MCQ hinges (slide 12).
  • Talk: how often students explain why a chart misleads, not just that it does.

Syllabus coverage

Enterprise information systems: Analysing data content addressed in these two lessons:

  • Differentiate between data and information (L1)
  • Describe the purpose of analysing data (L1)
  • Describe inputs, storage, transmission, processes and outputs in data analysis (L1)
  • Consider the social impacts and ethical and legal responsibilities in analysing data (L1)
  • Explore design principles and issues relevant to analysing data, including visualisation principles (L1, L2)
  • Collect and interpret data adhering to privacy and cybersecurity principles (L1, L2)
  • Represent and store data, including selecting appropriate data types and understanding data type limitations (L2: type check)
  • Use appropriate methods to collect, store, validate and verify qualitative and quantitative data, considering PII (L1, L2)
  • Generate a data visualisation to identify trends and outliers (L2)
  • Perform verification of datasets, calculations and outputs (L1 row totals, L2 Dataset A)
  • Evaluate their own project and that of their peers using predetermined requirements (L2 checklist feedback)
  • Select and use specialist terminology in context (both)
