Lesson 1 of 2 · Enterprise information systems: Analysing data

Is the world really ending?

A YouTuber surveyed 555 people and nearly two-thirds said the world is getting worse. Today we run our own survey, protect everyone’s privacy while we do it, turn the data into information, and find out whether a chart can tell the truth — or twist it.

CT5-DAT-01 CT5-DAT-02 CT5-SAF-01 CT5-COM-01

Learning intentions

From the syllabus: Enterprise information systems — Analysing data

We are learning to:

  • differentiate between data and information
  • describe inputs, storage, transmission, processes and outputs in data analysis
  • collect and interpret data adhering to privacy and cybersecurity principles
  • explore visualisation principles relevant to analysing data

Success criteria

I can:

  • differentiate between data and information using an example from our class survey
  • describe the input, storage, transmission, processing and output of our survey data
  • identify personally identifiable information (PII) and explain how our data collection protected privacy
  • evaluate a data visualisation against visualisation principles and explain how it could mislead

Worked example 3 min I do

From 555 answers to one sentence

Freddy did not publish 555 survey forms. He processed them and published one sentence. That sentence is information.

Data

555 separate answers: “worse”, “worse”, “better”, “same”, “worse” …

On its own, one answer tells you almost nothing.

→process:
count, compare,
add context

Information

“Nearly two-thirds of people said the world is getting worse.”

It has context (who, what), it answers a question, and someone can act on it.

Why analyse data at all? To answer a question or make a decision. Our question today: Does our class see the world more negatively than the data shows?

Safe and ethical data 3 min We do

Could anyone tell which slip is yours?

A survey is only honest if people feel safe answering it. Our design removes or blocks every piece of PII we could think of.

No names

The obvious one. Nobody’s name is on any slip.

Ticks, not writing

Handwriting can identify you, so almost every question is a tick box.

Shuffle and swap

Before we count, the slips are shuffled and handed out again. You hold a stranger’s answers, not your own.

Only totals are shared

We publish counts, never single slips. If only one person ticked a box, we talk about it carefully.

Case study

Freddy’s online survey

Freddy collected 555 responses online. An online form can quietly collect much more than the answers: an email address, a location, the time, the device and the IP address.

Discuss: Which of those could identify a person? What should a survey maker tell people before they answer?

Collect and process 6 min We do

Count the class

  1. The teacher shuffles the slips and hands one to each student. You now hold someone else’s answers.
  2. For each topic, hands up for the box ticked on the slip you’re holding.
  3. The teacher types each count, then we verify that every row adds up to the number of slips.
  4. Keep your slip face down. We need it again next lesson.
  • On your mini whiteboard, write one sentence of information from this chart. It must use a number and say who it is about.
  • A row adds up to one less than the number of slips. Give two reasons that could happen.

Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Measuring What Matters; Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water, Australian Energy Statistics.

It depends what you measure. “The world overall” is not one number, so no dataset can answer it. Some measures are improving and real problems remain. The video’s own conclusion is that the truth sits somewhere between “ending” and “fine”.

Data lesson: a question has to be specific and measurable before data can answer it.

Evaluate 7 min We do

Same data, four charts. Which ones can you trust?

Every chart below uses our class data. Check each one against the visualisation checklist, then reveal the answer. Booklet p. 4

  1. Honest headlineThe title says what the chart shows without exaggerating.
  2. Labels and sourceAxes, units and the data source are shown.
  3. Bars start at zeroOtherwise small differences look huge.
  4. Right chart typeBars compare categories, lines show change over time, dot plots show spread.
  5. Nothing cherry-pickedAll the relevant data is shown, not just the part that suits a story.
  6. Not colour aloneLabels or a legend explain what each colour means.
  7. Sample size shownThe number of responses (n) is given so readers can judge how far to trust it.

Your task 8 min You do

Fix a misleading chart

Your booklet has a chart headed “Smoking has COLLAPSED!” It uses real WHO figures: 26.2% of adults used tobacco in 2010 and 19.5% in 2024. Work at the level that suits you. Booklet pp. 4–5

Foundation · D–E

Check it against the list

Tick or cross each of the seven checklist items for the chart. Then finish the sentence: “The data shows ___, but the chart makes it look like ___.”

Core · C

Explain two problems

Name two ways the chart misleads and explain the effect each has on a reader. Rewrite the headline so it matches the data.

Extension · A–B

Measure the distortion

Calculate the real percentage fall, and the fall the bar heights suggest. Redraw the chart fairly on the grid. Who might want each version, and is it ethical to publish the misleading one?
