Theory: Planning an interactive marketing video

You will make a 60–90 second marketing video with one choice point. Every viewer's choice becomes a row of data. In these two lessons you plan the video, the data it collects, and how you will keep that data safe, fair and legal.

Identifying & Defining · Lesson 1 Researching & Planning · Lesson 2

By the end of Lesson 2 you will have:

  • Your product choice, a problem statement and a data question
  • A requirements table (functional and non-functional)
  • A branching storyboard with one choice point and a call-to-action
  • A data plan with consent wording, an accessibility checklist and a licence log
  • Your platform choice: HTML + JavaScript, Unity, or no-code + tally sheet

Lesson 1 · Analysis first

2. Take apart real interactive ads

Before you plan your own video, look at how companies already do it. Your teacher will show you two or three interactive ads or “choose your path” trailers. For each one, answer the same four questions.

The four analysis questions
QuestionWhat to look for
1. What choice is offered?The buttons, their labels and when they appear
2. What data could the company collect?The choice itself, how long the viewer took, whether they finished
3. What decision would that data inform?What the company might change: the next ad, the store page, the price
4. Is the viewer told that data is collected?A notice, a privacy link, or nothing at all

A 30-second social media ad for a soft drink shows three cans. A caption asks “Which flavour are you?” and the viewer taps one. A short clip for that flavour plays, then a “Find it in store” button appears.

  1. Choice offered: three flavour buttons, shown 8 seconds in.
  2. Data collected: which flavour was tapped, how long the viewer took, whether they tapped “Find it in store”, plus the viewer's account and location.
  3. Decision informed: which flavour to stock more of, and which flavour to show first in the next ad.
  4. Viewer told? Only through the platform's general privacy policy. Nothing in the ad itself says the tap is recorded.

Notice question 2: the platform can attach the viewer's account to the choice. That is PII. Your video will collect the choice but not who made it.

Watch the ad, then answer the four questions in your notebook. Use full sentences.

  1. The choice offered is ___. It appears ___ seconds in.
  2. The company could collect ___, ___ and ___.
  3. This data could help the company decide ___.
  4. The viewer is / is not told about data collection because ___.

Answer the four questions again. Then compare: which of the ads you looked at was most honest with its viewers about data? Which had the clearest choice buttons?

How did we get here? Companies have always wanted to know what audiences like. TV used paper diaries and phone surveys, which were slow and small. Websites began logging every click. Online ads then started testing two versions against each other to see which worked better. Interactive ads go one step further: the viewer's choice is the data, recorded the moment it happens.

3. Data vs information

This idea comes back in every lesson. Here is the example to remember:

Remember this one Gameplay clicked 33 times is data.
“Over half our viewers want to see gameplay first, so the store page should open on gameplay” is information.

Analysing data is the process that turns the first into the second. The purpose is always a decision: someone should be able to act on what you found.

Sort it

Choose Data or Information for each card, then check your sort.

  1. Gameplay — 33

  2. Most viewers choose Gameplay, so the store page should open on gameplay

  3. V-7K2Q, cp1, Story, 9.2

  4. Viewers who chose Multiplayer wishlisted the game more often than anyone else

  5. A list of 62 timestamps

  6. Story viewers take longest to decide, so the Story button may need a clearer label

  7. watchedToEnd = N

  8. Only 45 of 62 viewers reached the end, so the video may be too long

Quick test

Ask: “Could someone make a decision from this on its own?” If not, it is still data.

4. How the data flows

Every data system has inputs, processes, storage, transmission and outputs. Here is what happens inside your interactive video when one viewer makes one choice.

1 · Input

The viewer clicks a choice button. The program also notes when the buttons appeared.

→
2 · Process

The program works out the seconds taken to choose and builds one row of data.

→
3 · Storage

The row is saved on this computer: browser storage (HTML), a file (Unity) or paper (tally sheet).

→
4 · Transmission

After the carousel, the CSV file is downloaded and copied to your folder for analysis.

→
5 · Output

Straight away: the next video clip plays. Later: a chart, a prediction and a recommendation.

A real ad platform works the same way, with one big difference at step 4. The data is sent over the internet to the company's servers, for every viewer, instantly. That is why privacy matters so much more at that scale.

Lesson 2 · Researching & planning

6. Branching storyboard

Your video has one story choice point (cp1) and then a call-to-action (cp2). Keep it to 60–90 seconds. Reuse major-project footage, screen recordings or slides with voice-over. Don't film anything new.

Plan two designs, then pick one

Sketch two different storyboards for your product, for example a different question at the choice point or different button labels. Then check each one against your requirements and choose the one that meets more of them. Write one sentence explaining your choice.

No commas in button labels

Each label is saved into a CSV file, where commas separate the columns. “Yes, please” would split into two columns and break your data. Use “Yes please” instead.

Choose your platform

HTML video + JavaScript

Use the choice-logger.js starter. Good if you built a web app for your major project.

Unity

Use the ChoiceLogger.cs starter. Good if you built a game and want to reuse its scenes.

No-code + tally sheet

Use H5P or a similar tool, and record choices on the tally sheet. The data comes out in the same format.

All three platforms produce the same seven columns, so everyone can do the same analysis in Lesson 5.

Lesson 2 · Social awareness

7. Privacy and ownership

Every row your video saves is data about a real person. Before you build anything, decide what you will collect, who owns it, and how you will protect it.

Case study

The interactive film that remembered every choice

In 2018 Netflix released Black Mirror: Bandersnatch, an interactive film where viewers chose what the main character did next. In early 2019, a technology policy researcher at University College London used Europe's data protection law (GDPR) to ask Netflix what it had stored about him.

Netflix sent back a CSV file of every choice he had made, with timestamps. It said it used each viewer's choices to play the right branch of the story, and combined everyone's choices to improve this style of storytelling. Viewers had not been asked for permission, and Netflix did not say how long it would keep the data.

Discuss: Your video will also store a CSV of choices with timestamps. What is different about how you will collect it, and what should viewers be told?

Collect only what you need

Here is why each column in the class format exists. Every column must help answer the data question.

Columns collected and why
ColumnWhy we collect it
viewerIdLinks one viewer's two choices together. It's a random code like V-7K2Q, never a name.
videoIdTells the class's datasets apart
timestampPuts rows in order and spots duplicate rows
choicePoint, choiceThe answer to the data question
secondsToChooseShows which choice viewers found hardest
watchedToEndShows whether the video holds attention

Things we deliberately do not collect: names, gamer tags, emails, photos, age or location. None of them help answer the question, and all of them could identify someone.

Who owns the data?

Each choice comes from a viewer, but the file is created by your video. In this unit the viewer data belongs to the class project. It is used only for analysis in Lesson 5, and it is deleted at the end of the unit. Real companies make the same decision, but often write it in privacy policies most viewers never read. Every online choice leaves a data trail that someone else may keep.

Consent wording: clear or unclear?

Your video must tell viewers what is collected before they choose.

✓ Clear

“This video records which button you press and how long you take. It doesn't record your name or anything about you. Your answers help a Year 10 class improve this ad. Press Start if that's OK.”

✗ Unclear

“By continuing you agree to our data practices and the processing of interaction metrics in accordance with applicable policies.”

The clear version says what is collected, what is not collected, why, and how to say yes. The unclear version uses long words and gives no real choice.

Think about different viewers

  • Younger viewers may not understand data collection. Write consent wording a 12-year-old could follow.
  • Viewers learning English need short sentences and common words, on screen as well as spoken.
  • Viewers with disability need captions, keyboard access and readable buttons (see section 8).
  • Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples have the right to decide how data about their communities is collected and used. This is called Indigenous data sovereignty. If your product or footage involves a community, ask whose permission you need.

8. Accessibility checklist (POUR)

Accessible design follows four principles: Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust. Copy this checklist into your notebook. You will audit your finished video against it in Lesson 6.

POUR accessibility checklist for an interactive video
PrincipleCheckWho it helps
PerceivableAll speech has captionsDeaf viewers, people watching with the sound off, English learners
PerceivableButton text has strong contrast against its backgroundLow-vision viewers, anyone on a bright screen
OperableEvery choice button works with Tab and EnterKeyboard and switch users
OperableButtons are large enough to tap (at least 44 × 44 px)Touch-screen users, people with motor difficulties
OperableNo autoplay with sound; the video waits for StartScreen-reader users, anyone in a quiet room
UnderstandableButton labels say exactly what happens nextEveryone, especially first-time viewers
RobustThe video works on the carousel computers and browserEvery viewer in the carousel

9. Copyright and licences

Music, footage, images and fonts all belong to someone. Being free to watch online does not make something free to use. Check the licence before you use any asset.

Common licence types
LicenceCan you use it in your video?
All rights reserved (the default)No, unless the owner gives you written permission
Creative Commons BYYes, if you credit the creator
Creative Commons BY-NCYes for this class project, with credit. Not for a real commercial ad.
CC0 / public domainYes. Crediting the creator is still good practice.
Your own workYes. A classmate's major project needs their permission.

Course performance descriptors

What each grade looks like across this whole unit. Select a grade to highlight it.

A

Designs an interactive marketing product with a purposeful choice point, collects data ethically with clear consent and no PII, and cleans and validates it independently. Writes and justifies their own SQL queries (including a join), separates outliers from errors, and makes a prediction and decision supported by calculations. Evaluates the solution skilfully against requirements, accessibility (POUR) and legal responsibilities.

B

Builds a working interactive video that logs choices in the class format, cleans the data and fixes most planted problems. Runs the set queries and one of their own, produces a correct chart and prediction, and explains the difference between popularity and effectiveness. Evaluates thoroughly against requirements, privacy and copyright.

C

Builds an interactive video with one logged choice point, removes obvious errors from the data, and summarises it with spreadsheet functions and the set queries. Creates a suitable chart and a simple prediction, and evaluates the product against the requirements.

D

Builds a basic interactive video or uses the tally sheet, records viewer choices, and uses supplied data to create a basic chart. Identifies some privacy and accessibility features.

E

Identifies the data an interactive ad could collect. Follows the steps to record choices and partially summarises data with teacher support.
