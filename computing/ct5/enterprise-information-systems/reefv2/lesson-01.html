Lesson 1 of 3 · Enterprise information systems: Analysing data

Is the Great Barrier Reef recovering?

After six mass bleaching events in less than a decade, a wet summer cooled the water just in time. Surveys found more living coral across the northern and central reef. Today you become a reef surveyor, see why scientists check each other’s counts, and read 10 years of coral data that swings up and down.

CT5-DAT-01 CT5-DAT-02 CT5-THI-01 CT5-COM-01

Learning intentions

From the syllabus: Enterprise information systems — Analysing data

We are learning to:

  • describe inputs, storage, processes and outputs in data analysis
  • collect and interpret data using methods that validate and verify it
  • explore the applications of small and big datasets
  • generate a data visualisation to identify trends and outliers, and evaluate it against visualisation principles

Success criteria

I can:

  • describe how reef survey data moves from input to output
  • collect data with a point-count method and verify it against another surveyor
  • explain why scientists analyse a sample of reefs rather than every reef
  • identify trends, variability and outliers in a coral cover visualisation, and evaluate displays against visualisation principles

Worked example 3 min I do

How do you measure a reef the size of a country?

You can’t measure every coral. So the Australian Institute of Marine Science (AIMS) takes a sample. It has done this every year for 40 years. Booklet p. 3

  1. 1

    Input

    A diver is towed slowly around a reef on a board (a manta tow), estimating how much of the reef surface is live coral.

  2. 2

    Storage

    Every estimate is recorded with the reef, date and position, then stored in AIMS’ long-term database.

  3. 3

    Process

    121 reefs were surveyed in 2025–26. Statistical models combine them into an average for each of three regions, with a range of uncertainty.

    A sample: 121 reefs stand in for a whole reef system of around 3,000 reefs.

  4. 4

    Output

    An annual report each August: average hard coral cover for the Northern, Central and Southern reef.

Collect data 7 min We do

Be the surveyor: Quadrat A

A quadrat is a square photo of the reef from above. Scientists don’t trace every shape. They check what is directly under fixed points. Booklet p. 3

Live hard coral Dead coral Algae Sand
  1. Look only at the centre of each cross. What is directly under it?
  2. Tick each point that sits on live hard coral. Count your ticks.
  3. Coral cover = ticks ÷ 25 × 100. (Each point is worth 4%.)
  4. Write your answer as a percentage, then read it out when asked.
  • Everyone looked at the same photo. Why didn’t everyone get the same answer?
  • Look at the two outliers. What mistake might explain each one? Are they errors or real?
  • How could AIMS reduce surveyor disagreement? (Hint: training, clear rules, a second person checking a sample of the same photos.)

Count the class survey

The teacher shuffles the slips and hands them out. Hands up for the slip you are holding, not your own answer.

Why so low? Even a healthy reef is never 100% coral: there’s sand, rubble and algae between colonies. AIMS treats more than 30% hard coral cover as high.
Read the trend 6 min I do We do

Up, down, up: what is the trend?

Here are 10 years of AIMS results. Choose a region, then compare it with its long-term average (the dashed line).

Think aloud: Northern region

  1. Overall: from 11% (2017) to 35.1% (2026), so the trend is up and now above its long-term average of 27%.
  2. Swings: up 25.5 points from 2019 to 2024, then down 9.5 in a single year (2025), then up 5.1 (2026). The biggest one-year fall on record came straight after a record high.
  3. Missing years: no figure was published for 2018 or 2020, so the line has gaps. Joining them would invent data.
  4. The scientists’ warning: bigger, more frequent swings leave less time to recover. A trend can look fine while variability is rising.

Data can be revised

The 2024 report said northern cover was 39.5%. The 2025 report, using more surveys, put 2024 at 39.8%. Always record which report a number came from.

Evaluate 5 min We do

Same data, four charts. Which ones can you trust?

  1. Honest headlineThe title says what the chart shows without exaggerating.
  2. Labels and sourceAxes, units and the data source are shown.
  3. Honest scaleBars start at zero.
  4. Right chart typeLines for change over time, bars for comparing amounts, pies only for parts of one whole.
  5. Nothing cherry-pickedAll relevant years and regions are shown.
  6. Not colour aloneLabels or a legend explain what each colour means.
  7. Gaps and sample shownMissing data isn’t joined up, and readers can tell how much data is behind it.

How this lesson is graded

Today’s evidence sits against two course performance descriptor strands: developing computing solutions using computational, design and systems thinking (CT5-THI-01) and selecting data, media and processes to communicate information (CT5-DAT-02, CT5-COM-01). Click a grade to highlight it.

A

“Develops highly effective computing solutions using computational, design and systems thinking skills” and “selects relevant data, media and processes to effectively communicate information”. Today: you measure variability with evidence, explain how sampling and verification affect trust in the data, and explain exactly how each misleading chart distorts it.

B

“Develops effective computing solutions…” and “selects relevant data, media and processes to communicate appropriate information”. Today: you identify the biggest rises and falls, compare them with the long-term average, and explain why surveyors disagree.

C

“Develops sound computing solutions…” and “selects appropriate data, media and processes to communicate information”. Today: you estimate coral cover correctly, calculate yearly changes, and find at least two checklist problems.

D

“Develops basic computing solutions using computational or design or systems thinking skills” and “uses data to communicate basic information”. Today: you count coral points and plot the data.

E

“Uses data to communicate information in a very limited way”. Today: with support, you count some coral points and say whether coral went up or down. The computational, design and systems thinking strand has no Grade E descriptor, so only the communication strand is quoted. “Today” lines describe evidence for this lesson only.
