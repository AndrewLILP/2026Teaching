Teacher notes · not for students

Solar Data: demo double lesson

Two 55-minute lessons for Stage 5 Enterprise information systems: Analysing data. Lesson 1 is the observed demo: paper only, strict I do → We do → You do. Lesson 2 moves the same thinking into a spreadsheet and students build a dashboard. Each lesson page is also the slide deck: press P to present.

LessonBig ideaStudents produce
1 · How fast is solar growing?A trend is linear if the amount added stays the same, and exponential if the percentage stays the same. The pattern decides which prediction method makes sense. Charts can hide or exaggerate the pattern.Anonymous survey data, a hand-plotted graph with a 2030 prediction, an exit ticket
2 · Build a solar dashboardVerify before you calculate. Formulas turn data into information; IF turns information into a decision.A one-screen spreadsheet dashboard, peer feedback, one improvement

Before the lesson

  • Print: one student booklet per student (8 A4 pages). Print and cut one survey slip per student plus spares.
  • Gather: an envelope, mini whiteboards and markers, rulers and pencils (for plotting in Lesson 1).
  • Test the page on the teaching computer: open Lesson 1, press P, step to “Count the class”, click Load sample data on each widget, then Clear.
  • Use the same computer for both lessons: the class counts and guesses are saved in that browser. On another computer, re-type them from your photo of the board.
  • Lesson 2 files: put solar-data.xlsx (and the two CSVs for Google Sheets users) on your LMS or shared drive. Keep the solution to yourself.
  • Queue the video (youtube.com/watch?v=ugQ-EDbsS9s) and check both clip start times. Use ClickView or another approved download if your school has one, to avoid ads.
  • Keep the slips after the count in Lesson 1. You only need them again if you want to re-run a guess question.

If the tech fails

Lesson 1 runs on paper: tally on the board, plot the guesses as a dot plot by hand, and use booklet page 4 for the prediction task. In Lesson 2, the spreadsheet steps also work in Google Sheets from the CSVs.

Learning intentions and success criteria

Lesson 1 · We are learning to

  • turn data into information
  • recognise whether a trend is linear or exponential
  • make a prediction from data and know its limits
  • judge whether a data display informs or misleads

Lesson 1 · I can

  • explain the difference between data and information using a solar example
  • describe a trend as linear or exponential, using the amount added each year as evidence
  • make a prediction for 2030 and explain one reason it could be wrong
  • use the visualisation checklist to explain whether a chart shows data fairly

Lesson 2 · We are learning to

  • verify a dataset before using it
  • use spreadsheet formulas and functions to summarise data
  • make predictions and decisions with a spreadsheet
  • present information on a dashboard

Lesson 2 · I can

  • find and fix an error by checking units and the source
  • write a growth formula and fill it down a column
  • predict 2030 with FORECAST, and make a decision with IF
  • build a dashboard with a chart, three key numbers and a headline, then improve it from feedback

Answers

Lesson 1: clip

About 68 years to reach the first terawatt (the first practical silicon solar cell was made in 1954; 1 TW was reached in 2022), then about two years for each of the next two (2 TW in 2024, 3 TW in 2026). Solar can now supply about 9% of world electricity demand.

Lesson 1: sort, MCQs and sample data

  • Sort: data = 2378.04, the spreadsheet row, the single tick. Information = the three sentences.
  • MCQs: Q1 C · Q2 A · Q3 D · Q4 B.
  • Q2 sample guesses: validation rejects “ten” (type check) and 500 (range check, 0–200). 24 valid values: median 13.5 years, outliers 60 and 100. The actual answer is about 2. Most people think linearly.
  • Q3 sample predictions: rejects 3000 (range) and “soon” (type). Median 2036.5 (the page rounds it to 2037), outlier 2100. Industry forecast: about 2030.
  • Rooftop sample: 9 of 26 slips (35%) said yes, close to the national “one in three”. Our class is not a random sample. For example, students in apartments or rentals are less likely to have rooftop solar.

Lesson 1: world growth and predictions

  • Added each year: 31 GW in 2011 rising to 511 GW in 2025. The growth rate fell from about 77% (2010) to about 27% (2025).
  • World 2030 (explorer, from 2,378 GW in 2025): straight line (+333 GW a year) gives about 4,045 GW. Constant growth (27.3% a year) gives about 7,957 GW. SolarPower Europe expects more than 6,600 GW by 2030 on its own counting basis.

Lesson 1: the four displays

  • A fair. The headline “more than tripled” is supported (711 → 2,378 GW, about 3.3 times).
  • B cherry-picked 2000–2008, no source, misleading headline.
  • C an unlabelled log scale. “Slowing” is true of the rate but false of the amount: 2025 was a record year.
  • D the axis starts at 1,800 GW, so the 2025 bar is drawn about 8.6 times as tall as 2024. The real ratio is 1.27.

  • Pattern: since 2019 the increase is steady at about 4–5 GW a year, so the growth is linear. The growth % falls because the same amount is a smaller share of a bigger total.
  • Straight line: average increase 2019–2025 = (42.4 − 15.4) ÷ 6 = 4.5 GW, so 2030 ≈ 42.4 + 5 × 4.5 = about 65 GW.
  • Constant % (Extension): (42.4 ÷ 19.8)^(1/5) ≈ 1.165, so about 16.5% a year, and 42.4 × 1.1655 ≈ about 91 GW. This is less believable, because the % has fallen every year until 2025.
  • Who uses predictions: grid planners (such as the Australian Energy Market Operator), governments setting targets, and companies deciding where to build.

Lesson 2

  • Warm-up: 48 percentage points; 48 ÷ 27 ≈ 178% increase; 75 ÷ 27 ≈ 2.8 times, so “nearly tripling” is fair.
  • Planted error: Australia B18 (2016) is 5878 and should be 5.878. Megawatts were typed in place of gigawatts. Until it is fixed, the growth column shows about +116,700% for 2016 and −99.9% for 2017, and the chart spikes.
  • Boom years (above 50%): 2009 (301%), 2010 (231%), 2012 (77%), 2018 (54%). Students may suggest rebates and feed-in tariffs (around 2009–2012) or the first big solar farms (around 2018). Treat these as hypotheses to research, not facts the data shows.
  • Key numbers (checked by recalculating the solution workbook): latest 42.44 GW; average increase 2019–2025 = 4.50 GW; FORECAST 2030 = 64.5 GW; growth rate 2020–2025 = 16.5%; constant-growth 2030 = 91.1 GW; decision “On track” (64.5 ≥ 60); doubling time 4.5 years. The explorer’s straight line uses the 2020–2025 average, so it shows 65.1 GW. The small difference is a good discussion point.
  • MCQs: Q1 B · Q2 D · Q3 A.

Likely misconceptions

Students may think…Correct it with…
If the growth % falls, growth has stopped.Australia: the % falls every year until 2025, while the GW added stays at about 4.5. A falling rate on a bigger base can still mean big additions.
Exponential just means “fast”.Exponential means a constant percentage. Use the “added each year” bars: equal bars mean linear, growing bars mean exponential.
A prediction from a formula is a fact.Two formulas give 65 or 91 GW from the same data. Every prediction assumes the future copies the past.
Percentage points and percentages are the same thing.The warm-up: 27% → 75% is +48 percentage points but a 178% increase.
Different sources can’t both be right.The 2.4 / 3.0 TW table: different definitions and timing. Record the source and definition.
A strange value is always a real event.5878 is exactly 1,000 times too big, a units error. Check the source before explaining it.

Differentiation and grading

Tasks are tiered Foundation (D–E), Core (C) and Extension (A–B), matched to the Stage 5 course performance descriptors shown on each lesson page.

LessonDescriptor strandsEvidence to collect
1Selecting data, media and processes to communicate information · Safe, secure and ethical practices in the use of dataBooklet p. 3 (data system, privacy), pp. 4–5 (graph and prediction), exit ticket
2Developing computing solutions using computational, design and systems thinking · Communicating informationThe saved dashboard file, booklet pp. 7–8 (plan, feedback received, change made)
  • Literacy and EAL/D: sentence starters on every writing task; vocabulary is hand-written with an example; the font toggle includes OpenDyslexic.
  • Numeracy support: Foundation students plot on a pre-scaled grid; Core students only need subtraction and an average.
  • Extension: compound growth, doubling time (=LN(2)/LN(1+r)) and justifying a method. This is genuine Stage 5 Mathematics.

For observing colleagues

  • Gradual release: every new idea is modelled (I do), practised together with live class data (We do), then applied to a new dataset (Australia) on paper (You do). Each slide is labelled with its stage.
  • The hook is a misconception: the class’s guesses for the second terawatt are usually far too long, because people expect linear growth. The rest of the lesson explains why.
  • Transfer: students learn the pattern from the world data, then must decide for themselves that Australia’s pattern is different (linear) and choose a method to match.
  • Check-for-understanding moments: the sort hinge (slide 6), mini whiteboards (slide 9), thumbs vote with evidence (slide 10), checklist finger-vote (slide 12) and MCQ hinges (slide 13).
  • Privacy modelled: “Prefer not to say”, shuffle and swap, and totals only.

Syllabus coverage

  • Differentiate between data and information (L1)
  • Describe inputs, storage, transmission, processes and outputs in data analysis (L1)
  • Consider the social impacts and ethical and legal responsibilities in analysing data (L1)
  • Explore the applications of small and big datasets (L1: four million rows vs one row per year)
  • Describe how data analysis has evolved in response to people’s needs and opportunities (L1)
  • Explore design principles, including visualisation principles (L1, L2)
  • Collect and interpret data adhering to privacy principles (L1)
  • Represent and store data, including appropriate data types and structuring data systematically (L2)
  • Use appropriate methods to collect, store, validate and verify data (L1 guesses, L2 planted error)
  • Summarise data using formulas and functions of a spreadsheet (L2)
  • Filter, group and sort data, including conditional formatting (L2)
  • Present data and make predictions and decisions using a spreadsheet, including a dashboard and decision formulas (L2)
  • Generate a data visualisation to identify trends and outliers (L1, L2)
  • Evaluate sourced data (L2: three sources disagree)
  • Perform verification of datasets, calculations and outputs (L2)
  • Evaluate peers’ projects using predetermined requirements (L2)
