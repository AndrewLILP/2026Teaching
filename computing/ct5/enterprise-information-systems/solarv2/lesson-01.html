Lesson 1 of 2 · Enterprise information systems: Analysing data

How fast is solar growing?

It took the world 68 years to install its first terawatt of solar panels, and about two years to add the second. Today we survey our class, read the data on solar growth, decide whether it is growing in a straight line or speeding up, and test whether a chart and a prediction can be trusted.

CT5-DAT-01 CT5-DAT-02 CT5-SAF-01 CT5-COM-01

Learning intentions

From the syllabus: Enterprise information systems — Analysing data

We are learning to:

  • differentiate between data and information
  • describe inputs, storage, transmission, processes and outputs in data analysis, and explore applications of small and big datasets
  • collect and interpret data adhering to privacy principles
  • generate and evaluate data visualisations to identify trends and make predictions

Success criteria

I can:

  • differentiate between data and information using solar capacity data
  • describe how solar installation data moves from input to output, and explain why millions of records make a big dataset
  • explain how our survey protected household privacy
  • identify a linear or exponential trend, make a prediction with one limitation, and evaluate a display against visualisation principles

Worked example 2 min I do

Where does Australia’s solar data come from?

More than 4 million Australian homes have rooftop solar. Every one is a row of data somewhere. Booklet p. 3

  1. 1

    Input

    An installer records each new system: its size, the postcode and the date.

  2. 2

    Storage

    A national database at the Clean Energy Regulator: millions of rows.

  3. 3

    Transmission

    Totals are shared with energy agencies, then reported to IRENA, an international agency.

  4. 4

    Process

    Add up the systems for each year, convert units, and check for errors.

    Big data: an Excel worksheet stops at 1,048,576 rows. Four million rows need a database.

  5. 5

    Output

    Small, tidy datasets like ours: one row per year, ready to chart.

Safe and ethical data 2 min We do

Your roof is personal information

Question 1 is about your home, not just you. That makes it more sensitive than a guess.

“Prefer not to say”

Nobody should have to share details about their family or home. A choice to opt out is part of a fair survey.

Postcode, not address

National solar data is published by postcode. A street address plus a system size would point to one house.

Shuffle and swap

We count hands for the slip you are holding, not your own answer.

Only totals are shared

We show percentages for the class, never one person’s answer.

Read the trend 5 min I do We do

Is solar growing in a straight line, or speeding up?

Look at the same world data three ways. Then decide: does the world add the same amount each year (linear), or grow by roughly the same percentage, so the amount keeps getting bigger (exponential)?

Think aloud

  1. Total chart: the line curves upward. That is the first clue it isn’t linear.
  2. Added each year: the bars get taller almost every year, from 31 GW in 2011 to 511 GW in 2025. Linear growth would give bars of equal height.
  3. Log scale: a straight line would mean a steady percentage. Ours bends, so the growth rate is slowing even though the amount added is still rising.

Evaluate 5 min We do

Same data, four charts. Which ones can you trust?

Every chart below uses the same world solar data. Test each one against the checklist, then reveal the answer.

  1. Honest headlineThe title says what the chart shows without exaggerating.
  2. Labels and sourceAxes, units and the data source are shown.
  3. Honest scaleBars start at zero; any log scale is clearly labelled.
  4. Right chart typeLines for change over time, bars for comparing amounts.
  5. Nothing cherry-pickedThe whole relevant time period is shown.
  6. Not colour aloneLabels or a legend explain what each colour means.
  7. Sample size or coverage shownReaders can tell how much data, and whose, is in the chart.
