Lesson 1 of 12 · 50 minutes

Deconstructing photo modes

Over the next 12 lessons you'll add a photo journal to your own game. While hiking, players will be able to frame a shot, capture it, and keep it in a journal that unlocks Steam achievements and works on Steam Deck. Before writing any code, we'll look at how professional games solve this problem, then turn what we find into requirements.

Identifying & defining Researching & planning

Teaching advice

Timing: hook 5 min · vocabulary 7 min · deconstruct 12 min · DART 8 min · requirements and tasks 12 min · checkpoint and reflection 6 min.

Prepare: 2–3 short gameplay clips (1–2 min each) showing a photo mode in action, for example Alba: A Wildlife Adventure, TOEM or New Pokémon Snap. Students who have played a game with a photo mode can analyse that game instead. Steam is not needed in this lesson.

Students need: their own game project with a working on-foot controller. They don't open it today, but the requirements they write are for their game, not a generic one.

Learning intentions

By the end of this lesson I can:

  • break down an existing photo mode into its inputs, processes and outputs
  • tell the difference between functional requirements, non-functional requirements and constraints
  • write testable requirements for a photo journal in my own game, including Steam Deck requirements.
Success criteria: I have written at least five functional and three non-functional requirements for my game's photo journal, and each one has a way to test it.

Why photo modes?

Many games sold on Steam include a way to take photos. In some, like Alba: A Wildlife Adventure, photographing animals and logging them is the main goal. In others, a photo mode is an extra feature players use to share moments from the game.

A good photo mode feels effortless to the player, but it is a small system with many moving parts: a special camera, input that changes meaning, images saved to disk, a journal that remembers everything, and often rewards like achievements. That makes it an ideal feature to study, because it touches almost every part of the computing domains: programming, data, user interfaces and project management.

Here is the whole feature as an input–process–output (IPO) chain. Every lesson in this unit builds one part of it.

Input

Player presses the photo button while hiking, aims with the stick, presses capture.

→
Process

Check photo mode is allowed, hide the HUD, capture the screen, save the image, update the journal.

→
Output

Shutter feedback, a new journal entry, and possibly an achievement unlock.

Your game, your journal

Everyone builds this into their own game. A forest trail, a city walk or a moon base are all fine, as long as your player can move around on foot.

Deconstruct a photo mode

Professional developers study existing products before designing their own. You'll watch a short clip of a photo mode and break it down into the parts below. Focus on what the game does, not whether you like it.

Worked example: from observation to requirement Observation When the player raises the camera, the map and button prompts fade away. Requirement The HUD is hidden while photo mode is active and does not appear in saved photos. Test Take a photo, open the saved image, and confirm no HUD elements are visible.

Notice the pattern: an observation describes one game, a requirement describes what our system must do, and a test proves it. A requirement you can't test is only a wish.

Read like a developer

The text below is a developer's description of building a camera for a hiking game called Ridgeline. It's full of requirements, but they are mixed together the way they usually are in real discussions.

DART · text marking · 8 min

Copy or print the text. Mark each idea with one colour:

Functional: what it does Non-functional: how well Constraint: project limit

When we added a camera to Ridgeline, we started with one rule: taking a photo should never break the calm of the walk. Players press Y to raise the camera, and the HUD fades out so only the landscape is visible. The left stick moves the camera within a small circle around the hiker. Pressing A takes the photo, plays a shutter sound and briefly flashes the screen white. Every photo is added to the hiker's journal with the date and the name of the trail.

The game must not stutter when a photo is taken. Playtesters noticed a half-second freeze in an early build, so we moved saving into the background. Because we were releasing on Steam Deck, every menu had to work with a controller alone, and journal text had to be readable on a handheld screen. We had six weeks and no money for paid plugins, so we used Unity's built-in screenshot tools. The journal holds 60 photos. When it is full, the game asks the player to remove one before taking another.

Functional: pressing Y raises the camera · the HUD fades out · the left stick moves the camera within a small circle · pressing A takes the photo with a sound and flash · each photo is added to the journal with date and trail name · when full, the game asks the player to remove a photo.

Non-functional: taking a photo never breaks the calm (usability) · no stutter when capturing (performance) · every menu works with a controller alone (usability and accessibility) · journal text is readable on a handheld screen (usability).

Constraint: six weeks · no money for paid plugins · must release on Steam Deck.

Up for debate: is "the journal holds 60 photos" a constraint or a functional requirement? It's a design decision the team made to limit storage, so you can argue either way. What matters is that you can justify your choice.

Steam Deck as a requirement

Case study

Designing for a handheld

Steam Deck is a handheld PC. Valve reviews games and gives them a compatibility rating, and "Verified" is the highest. A verified game must work using the Deck's built-in controls, without needing a keyboard or mouse. Its on-screen button prompts must match the Deck's buttons, its text must be easy to read on the small 1280 × 800 screen, and if the player needs to type, the game must bring up an on-screen keyboard.

None of these are new features. They are qualities of features we're already building. That makes most of them non-functional requirements, and each one changes how we design. For example, if players can caption photos, typing must work without a keyboard.

Discuss: A classmate says, "We'll worry about Steam Deck at the end, once the journal works." Using the caption example, explain why that plan could cost them extra time later.

Defining

R3 test: take a photo in a named area, add a caption, quit and reload, then check the entry still shows all three pieces of information.

R4 test: take 10 photos in a row while watching the frame rate counter (or the Unity Profiler). The frame rate should not drop sharply on capture.

R6: only Unity's built-in tools and free packages may be used. Other options include "the feature must be finished in 12 lessons" or "the game must run on Steam Deck hardware".

Your task

Choose the level that challenges you. You can move up a level once you finish.

Foundation

Sort the statements

Label each statement F (functional), N (non-functional) or C (constraint):

  • The player can delete a photo.
  • The journal opens in under one second.
  • The project is due in week 10.
  • Photos show the area name.
  • Button prompts match the controller in use.
  • Only free assets may be used.

Then write one functional requirement of your own.

Core

Requirements for your game

Copy the requirements table and adapt it to your game. Write at least five functional and three non-functional requirements.

  • Include at least one Steam Deck requirement.
  • Give every requirement a test.
  • Name your game's real areas, e.g. "Lookout Point".
Extension

Write a use case

Write the use case "Hiker photographs a landmark and adds it to the journal" as numbered steps.

  • Add two alternative flows: the journal is full, and Steam isn't running.
  • Rank your requirements as Must, Should or Could have, and justify your top two.

Checkpoint

Answer all six. The first two are Foundation, the middle two are Core and the last two are Extension.

1. Which statement is a functional requirement for a photo journal?
2. A requirement says, "Photos must save without the game stuttering." What type of requirement is this?
3. A developer limits the photo camera to a small circle around the hiker. What problem does this most likely prevent?
4. Which test best checks the requirement "The HUD never appears in a saved photo"?
5. A Steam Deck Verified game must work with only the built-in controls. Which requirement best supports this for photo captions?
6. In the use case "Hiker takes a photo", the journal is already full. Which is the best alternative flow?
