Evaluation and showcase

Twelve lessons ago, your photo journal was a list of requirements. Today you'll judge the finished feature honestly against that list, think carefully about the data it creates and where that data goes, watch a classmate use it without your help, and present it the way a studio would present a feature on a store page.

Testing & evaluating
Last lesson: you wrote automated unit tests, chose normal, boundary and invalid data, and logged defects by severity. Your test results, audit rows and defect log are today's evidence.

Teaching advice

Timing: recap and vocabulary 3 min · requirements evaluation 10 min · ethics 9 min · accessibility and credits 3 min · peer playtest 15 min · showcase 5 min · checkpoint 5 min. The unit reflection can be finished at home.

Playtest pairs: pair students whose games they haven't seen. Each student is tester once and observer once, about 6 minutes each. Print the task card if you can, so it isn't on the tester's screen.

Assessment: the requirements evaluation, ethics response and unit reflection together make strong evidence for the descriptors on evaluating, ethical data practices and iterative processes.

Learning intentions

By the end of this lesson I can:

  • evaluate a solution against its requirements, using evidence
  • explain what data my feature creates, where it goes and who can see it
  • gather feedback from a real user and decide what to change first
  • communicate the value of my work to an audience.
Success criteria: my evaluation table gives evidence for every requirement, I've answered the ethics questions with a recommendation, I've run and responded to a playtest, and I've written a store-page description of my feature.

Key vocabulary

Evaluating

Evaluate against requirements

An evaluation isn't "I think it's good". It goes back to the requirements you wrote in Lesson 1 and asks, for each one: was it met, and how do I know? Here's how the shared requirements map to evidence you've already collected.

Req.RequirementEvidence to look atMet?Next step

R2The HUD never appears in a saved photoT7
R3Each photo is stored with caption, location and dateT11, T12, T19, T37, T40

R6Only Unity's built-in tools and free assetsYour credits list (see below)

Copy the table, add your own requirements from Lesson 1, and fill in the last two columns. Use Fully, Partly or Not met.

Worked example: an honest evaluation sentence Weak "R4 is met because capturing photos works well." Strong "R4 is partly met. In audit row A7, the worst frame while capturing was 48 ms, above the 33 ms budget for 30 fps, so players may notice a brief hitch. Moving the file write to a background thread (the Lesson 4 Extension) is the planned fix." Why it's stronger It gives a judgement, a measurement, a comparison with a target, the effect on the player, and a specific next step.

"Partly met" with good evidence is a better evaluation than "fully met" with none. Assessors and real employers both value honesty backed by data.

Ethics discussion · pairs · 6 min

Should the game ask first?

In Lesson 9, the Steam version sends every photo to the player's Steam screenshot library automatically. The player was never asked. Most players probably like it, but a photo might show a caption they wrote privately, and their Steam settings might share screenshots more widely than they realise.

  1. List the stakeholders (everyone affected): the player, your studio, Valve, and anyone else you can think of. What does each one want?
  2. Should sending photos to Steam be opt-in (off until chosen), opt-out (on until turned off), or not offered at all? Justify your choice.
  3. If your game were for younger players, would your answer change? Why?

Write: a 3–4 sentence recommendation to your "studio" about this feature, mentioning at least two stakeholders.

Notice how much the Lesson 5 design protects players already: photos and journal data stay on their own computer, nothing is uploaded unless the Steam version is running, and the save can't be corrupted by a crash. Privacy by design means making these choices from the start, not adding them at the end.

Accessibility and credits

Accessibility check

  • Is anything shown only by colour? (The Lesson 10 readout uses red and green. It's a developer tool, but would you ship it?)
  • Does the shutter sound have a visual partner (the flash), for players who play with sound off?
  • Can every action be done with one input device?
  • Is all text at least 24 px at 1280 × 800 (audit row A5)?

Credits and licences (R6)

  • List every asset you didn't make: sounds, fonts, models, icons.
  • For each one, record where it came from and its licence.
  • Check whether the licence requires a credit. If it does, add it to your game's credits.
Testing

Peer playtest

Every test so far was run by the person who built the feature, who already knows where everything is. A real player doesn't. This is user acceptance testing.

Playtest rules · 6 minutes per person

  1. Tester: use only the gamepad. Think aloud: say what you're looking for and what you expect to happen.
  2. Observer (the game's developer): don't help, point or explain. If the tester is stuck for 30 seconds, note it, then say "What would you try next?"
  3. Observer records: where the tester hesitated, anything they tried that didn't work, and how long the whole task took.

Task card (give this to the tester)

Without any help:

  1. Take a photo in two different places.
  2. Open your journal and give one photo a caption.
  3. Delete the other photo.
  4. Return to the game.
Tester feedback form1 (no) – 5 (yes)

Respond to feedback · 4 min

What do you change first?

List every change suggested by your observations and the form. For each, rate the impact (how much it would improve the experience) and the effort (how long it would take) as high or low. Changes with high impact and low effort come first. Pick one and, if time allows, make it now.

Showcase

Steam store pages describe each game's features in a few short paragraphs, with the best screenshots beside them. Write yours.

Store page feature · 5 min

  1. Choose the best photo from your own journal. It's your feature's screenshot.
  2. Write a heading (up to 6 words) and a paragraph (40–70 words) describing the photo journal to a player. Focus on what they'll do and feel, not how you coded it.
  3. List your feature's three Steam-ready qualities, e.g. "Full controller support".

Benchmark

Is it $20 polish?

The unit's goal was the polish of a $20 Steam game. Compare honestly: what does your feature already do that commercial photo modes do? What do the best ones have that yours doesn't yet, such as filters, a camera roll animation or sharing? This isn't about matching a studio of dozens of people. It's about knowing exactly where your work stands and what "next" looks like.

Your task

Foundation

Judge and share

  • Complete the evaluation table for R1–R6 with evidence.
  • Take part in the playtest as tester and observer.
  • Write your store-page heading and paragraph.
Core

Evaluate fully

  • Evaluate every requirement, including your own, writing at least two "strong" evaluation sentences.
  • Write the ethics recommendation.
  • Prioritise playtest feedback by impact and effort, and make one change.
Extension

Privacy by design

  • Implement your ethics recommendation: add a "Send photos to Steam" setting that PlatformLink checks before calling AddScreenshot. Store the choice in PlayerPrefs.
  • Write pseudocode, a test case, and a short note on what your default is and why.

Checkpoint

Answer all six. The first two are Foundation, the middle two are Core and the last two are Extension.

1. Which is the best evidence that R2, "The HUD never appears in a saved photo", has been met?
2. In a peer playtest, why shouldn't the developer help the tester?
3. The Steam version sends every photo to the player's Steam screenshot library automatically. Which change best respects the player's choice?
4. Playtests suggest two changes. A: enlarge text that 4 of 5 testers struggled to read (small effort). B: add photo filters (large effort, asked for by 1 tester). Which should come first?
5. The Lesson 10 readout shows good performance in green and poor in red, with no other difference. Why is that an accessibility problem?
6. A project passes 38 of 40 tests, and both failures are minor defects. Which is the most accurate evaluation?

E

Works safely with data. Identifies that photos and captions are the player's data.
