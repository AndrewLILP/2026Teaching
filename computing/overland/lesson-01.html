What makes a trail game work?

Before the party leaves for the coast, we're pulling apart the games that made this genre — what state they track, what they randomise, and why players keep clicking "ford the river" even when it usually goes badly.

CT5-THI-01 CT5-DPM-01
Warm-up

A wagon, a river, and eleven variables

The original Oregon Trail (1971) is one of the oldest resource-management simulations still played today. Under the hood it's simple: a handful of numbers (food, health, money, distance travelled, days passed) and a loop that changes those numbers a little each day, then occasionally throws a random event at the player — a broken wagon wheel, a dose of dysentery, a river with no bridge.

That's the entire genre in one sentence: track some state, change it over time, interrupt it with randomness. Every game we build this unit follows that same shape.

Analysis activity

Pull two games apart

Play (or watch a playthrough of) two text-based or resource-management simulations — Oregon Trail and one other of your choice (Organ Trail, Don't Starve's early survival loop, or a mobile idle-management game all work). For each, answer the analysis questions below.

Analysis activity

Compare the two games

  1. What pieces of information does the game track about the player's situation? List everything you can spot — this is the game's state.
  2. What changes automatically every "turn" or "day", without the player doing anything?
  3. What changes only because of a player decision?
  4. What random events show up? How often, and how severe?
  5. How does the game tell the player what just happened? Is the feedback clear enough to act on?
  6. How does the game end — and how many different ways can it end?

Bring your answers to the Project Notebook — Phase 1 asks for this comparison in full.

Vocabulary

Words you'll need this unit

Click each card to flip it.

State Show definition
All the information a program is currently holding — in this game, the party's food, health, distance, and days. State changes as the program runs.
Resource management Show definition
A game genre built around balancing limited quantities (food, money, time) against ongoing demands, usually under some time pressure.
Random event Show definition
An outcome the program selects unpredictably, usually from a weighted set of possibilities, rather than something the player controls directly.
Simulation Show definition
A program that models how a real or imagined system behaves over time, so a user can explore "what if" without the real-world consequences.
Replayability Show definition
How much reason a player has to play again. Randomness and branching outcomes are two of the cheapest ways to build it in.
Brief Show definition
A written statement of what a solution must do, who it's for, and what constraints it must work within — the target you design and test against.

Defining the solution

The Overland brief

Over the next five lessons, you're building a text-based Python simulation that runs entirely in the console. Here's what it must do, at minimum, by the end of Lesson 6.

RequirementDetail
Track stateFood, health, wagon condition, and distance travelled, held in a dictionary
Advance over timeA loop that runs one "day" per iteration until the party arrives or runs out of a critical resource
Respond to the playerAt least one meaningful choice per day (e.g. pace, ration size)
Include randomnessAt least three distinct random events, each affecting state differently
End clearlyAt least two distinct endings (arrival, and at least one failure state)
Communicate clearlyConsole output a player can understand without reading the code
Note: this brief is deliberately open on theme. "Overland" is the default setting, but if you'd rather simulate a different journey — a supply run, an expedition, a delivery route — the same requirements apply. The state and structure matter more than the setting.

How this lesson is graded

This lesson's evidence sits mainly against CT5-THI-01 (computational, design and systems thinking) — specifically, how thoroughly you analyse the existing games and how clearly you define the brief.

A

Your analysis identifies subtle design decisions in both games (e.g. why an event is weighted the way it is) and your brief anticipates edge cases before you've written any code.

B

Your analysis covers all six questions in detail for both games, and your brief is specific enough that someone else could build to it.

C

Your analysis answers all six questions for both games, and your brief restates the requirements in your own words.

D

Your analysis answers most of the six questions for at least one game, and you can describe what the brief is asking for.

E

You can identify at least one thing the game tracks and one random event it uses. CT5-THI-01 has no Grade E descriptor in the syllabus — this line describes minimum evidence for this lesson only, not a syllabus quote.
