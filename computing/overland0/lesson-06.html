Warm-up

You've arrived. Now what?

A working, tested trail simulator is a genuinely solid piece of software — five lessons of state design, loops, functions, randomness, and testing, all correctly wired together. But "correct" and "creative" are different achievements, and the CPD descriptors treat them that way: Grade C asks for a sound solution; Grades A and B specifically ask for creativity and innovation in the design.

Creativity isn't the same as "add more stuff." Today gives you a deliberate way to generate a genuinely new idea, rather than just piling on features until something sticks.

Tool

Four ways to be creative on purpose

Instead of staring at your finished game hoping for inspiration, try mutating it along one of four axes. Each one takes something you've already built and deliberately changes it in a specific direction.

Contrast Flip an assumption the game currently makes. Right now, running low on a resource is the threat — what if too much of something became the problem instead? A wagon overloaded with salvaged supplies moving too slowly to outrun a storm, say. Combination Merge the trail mechanic with something from a completely different genre. A trail-management layer between rounds of a different mini-game — resources earned on the trail unlock choices in a totally separate system. Constraint Remove something the player currently relies on, and see what that forces. What if the player never sees exact numbers — only vague descriptions like "getting low" or "critical"? Suddenly every decision is judgement under uncertainty, not arithmetic. Context Keep every mechanic identical, but change the setting entirely. Same state dictionary, same loop, same event structure — but it's a supply run across a flooded city, or a delivery route through a besieged town, instead of a wagon on the Oregon Trail.

Pick one axis. Trying to apply all four at once in one lesson is how solid ideas turn into unfinished messes — a single well-executed change beats four half-finished ones, both for your grade and for the game itself.

Plan your extension

Before touching code, answer these three questions. This is the same planning discipline from Lesson 2, applied to a smaller change:

  • Which axis are you using, and what specifically will change?
  • Which part of your state dictionary or which function does this actually touch? Naming it now stops you discovering mid-build that the change needs to ripple through code you didn't expect.
  • How will you test it? Same discipline as Lesson 5 — decide the expected result before you build, not after.
Communication

Presenting to an audience

A working game and a good presentation of that game are different skills. Your teacher and your classmates want different things from watching you present — selecting the right data and media for each audience is itself part of CT5-COM-01, not just a formality before the "real" mark is decided.

SegmentTimeWhat to show
Live demo~2 minPlay a full run, including your extension firing at least once
Design decision~1 minName which of the four axes you used and why you chose it over the others
Test evidence~1 minShow one real bug you found in Lesson 5 testing and how you fixed it
Questions~1 minBe ready to explain what any one of your functions does, line by line, if asked
Note: the "test evidence" segment isn't optional padding — presenting a bug you found and fixed is stronger CT5-EVL-01 evidence than presenting a game that "just worked," because it shows you actually tested rather than got lucky.
Vocabulary

Words you'll need this unit

Click each card to flip it.

Scope creep Show definition
When a project keeps growing beyond its original plan, feature by feature, until nothing is actually finished. The single-axis rule today exists to prevent it.
Polish Show definition
Refining something that already works — better wording, smoother pacing, clearer feedback — rather than adding new functionality.
Critique Show definition
Structured, specific feedback aimed at improving a piece of work — different from praise or criticism because it points at something actionable.
Audience Show definition
Who a piece of communication is actually for — different audiences need different information presented in different ways to understand the same work.
Iteration (design sense) Show definition
Revisiting and improving a solution in cycles, rather than trying to get it perfect in one pass — the whole unit has been one iteration after another.

Your task

Today's task, by tier

Foundation · D–E

Polish, don't extend

Pick two pieces of feedback text and rewrite them to be clearer. Fix one bug you haven't already fixed. Prepare a short demo.

Core · C

Apply one axis

Choose one creativity axis, plan it using the guided template, build it, and test it against a stated expected result. Prepare the full presentation.

Extension · A–B

Justify your axis against the alternatives

Do the Core task, then in your notebook explain why you chose this axis over the other three — what would each of the others have changed instead, and why is your choice the strongest fit for this game specifically?

How this lesson is graded

Evidence here spans CT5-THI-01 (creativity and innovation in your design) and CT5-COM-01 (selecting the right information for your audience when presenting).

A

Your extension shows genuine creativity — it changes how the game feels to play, not just what numbers it tracks — and you can articulate exactly why you rejected the other three axes for this project.

B

Your extension is a clear, deliberate application of one creativity axis, correctly implemented and tested, and your presentation clearly explains the design decision behind it.

C

You've designed and implemented a working user interface and experience across the unit, and your presentation covers all four checklist segments.

D

You've implemented basic elements of the interface (clear enough console feedback) and you present a working demo of your game.

E

You can identify one element of your game's console output that helps the player understand what's happening.
