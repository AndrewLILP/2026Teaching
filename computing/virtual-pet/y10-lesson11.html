Test & Evaluate Your Pet Family

Your sprint logs caught bugs as you went — useful, but incidental. This lesson is deliberate: a formal test plan covering Pet, Dog, your second pet type, and FoodBowl together, a desk check tracing your own logic by hand, and a written evaluation against the Y10 Pet Brief you wrote in Lessons 3–4. No new C# today — just proof your pet family actually does what you designed it to do.

Testing & Evaluating

Formal Test Plan

Unlike your sprint test tables, which tested one class at a time as you built it, this table deliberately tests the same kind of behaviour across every class together — including your two different IInteractable implementations — because that's the only way to catch something that quietly broke on one class but not another.

Requirement (from your brief) Test case Your actual result (write in notebook)
Win Condition Raise every core stat above your brief's win threshold, on any pet type Record actual result →
Lose Condition — Pet Set hunger to 0 in the Inspector, confirm no methods change stats afterward Record actual result →
Lose Condition — Dog With alive false, press Fetch Record actual result →
Lose Condition — second type With alive false, trigger its unique method Record actual result →
Overridden methods Trigger Dog's and your second type's overridden methods; confirm both the inherited and new behaviour fire Record actual result →
Interface polymorphism Interact with a Pet-family object, then with FoodBowl; confirm each runs its own distinct logic Record actual result →
Encapsulation Try to write pet.hunger = 100; from an unrelated script Record actual result →

Desk Check

A desk check traces a sequence of method calls by hand, predicting each value before you ever press Play — it's how you catch a logic error that testing in the Editor might not show you clearly. Here's a worked example, starting from hunger = 50:

Step Action Hunger before Expected hunger after Alive?
1Interact()5070true
2Interact()7090true
3Interact()90100 (capped)true
4Interact()100100 (no change)true

Your Turn

In your notebook, desk-check your own Lose Condition sequence for one pet type — a series of actions that eventually sets alive to false, and one attempted action afterward that should be blocked. Then run the actual sequence in Unity and confirm your prediction matched.

Testing & Evaluating

Evaluate Against Requirements

Testing tells you what happened. Evaluating asks whether what happened actually satisfies the brief. Write a short paragraph for each:

🔍 Evaluation Prompts
  1. Win & Lose Conditions: do they behave exactly as your brief specified, on every pet type and FoodBowl, with no exceptions?
  2. Shared vs Unique: did your original split hold up, or did something end up in the wrong place once you'd actually built it?
  3. Interaction Design: does FoodBowl's behaviour genuinely differ from the pet family's, the way you planned in Lessons 3–4?
  4. Asset Store Shortlist: did every asset you actually used match what you originally listed, with its licence checked?
  5. Non-functional quality: do bars and TextMeshPro elements feel immediate, with no visible delay anywhere in your project?

CPD Grade Descriptors — Stage 5

These are the NESA Course Performance Descriptors for Stage 5 Computing Technology, used to report your achievement at the end of Year 10. They apply across every lesson in Pet Lab — not just this one. Tap a grade to expand it.

A

Demonstrates an extensive understanding of the influence of enterprise, innovation and automation on the evolution of computing technology; skilfully applies appropriate iterative processes to produce computing solutions; develops highly effective computing solutions using computational, design and systems thinking skills; selects and applies safe, secure and ethical practices in the use of data; skilfully develops, tests and implements technically concise algorithms in a general-purpose programming language; demonstrates creativity and innovation in the design and implementation of user interfaces to create engaging user experiences; selects relevant data, media and processes to effectively communicate information in a range of contexts.

B

Demonstrates a thorough understanding of the influence of enterprise, innovation and automation on the evolution of computing technology; applies appropriate iterative processes to produce computing solutions; develops effective computing solutions using computational, design and systems thinking skills; selects and applies safe, secure and ethical practices in the use of data; develops, tests and implements functional algorithms in a general-purpose programming language; demonstrates creativity in the design and implementation of user interfaces to create engaging user experiences; selects relevant data, media and processes to communicate appropriate information in a range of contexts.

C

Demonstrates a sound understanding of the influence of enterprise, innovation and automation on the evolution of computing technology; applies iterative processes to produce computing solutions; develops sound computing solutions using computational, design and systems thinking skills; applies safe, secure and ethical practices in the use of data; develops common algorithms and implements them in a general-purpose programming language; designs and implements user interfaces to create user experiences; selects appropriate data, media and processes to communicate information in a range of contexts.

D

Demonstrates a basic understanding of the influence of enterprise and innovation on the evolution of computing technology; uses processes to produce computing solutions; develops basic computing solutions using computational or design or systems thinking skills; uses data safely and responsibly; develops basic algorithms and/or implements them in a general-purpose programming language; implements basic elements of user interface design to support user experiences; uses data to communicate basic information.

E

Identifies the evolution of, and/or innovations in, computing technology; identifies processes that may produce a computing solution; works safely with data; follows algorithms and/or partially implements them in a general-purpose programming language; identifies elements of user interfaces that contribute to user experiences; uses data to communicate information in a very limited way.

Where this lesson sits Lessons 11–12 are almost entirely evidence for the evaluation bullets in these descriptors — a formal test plan, a correct desk check, and a written evaluation against your own brief are exactly what distinguishes "tested" from merely "worked when I tried it once."
