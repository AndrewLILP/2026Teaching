Identifying & Defining

Your Brief

Studio memo: "Every pet PixelPals has ever shipped started as a one-page brief, not a finished game. We need to know what your pet is called, what it looks like, which three stats it tracks, and exactly what counts as a win and a loss — before your team writes a single block. Pitches without a clear brief get sent back."

A brief is a short, specific plan that a whole team (or a future version of yourself) could build from without guessing. Vague briefs lead to vague pets — if you can't say exactly what happens when hunger hits 0, you won't know what to build in Lesson 9. Today's job is to remove every guess.

Identifying & Defining

Writing Your Pet Brief

Your brief has four parts. Use your Lesson 1–2 notebook evidence to inform each one — you're deliberately reusing and improving on decisions you already analysed.

Part 1 · Concept

Give your pet a name and a species (real, imaginary, or invented). In one or two sentences, describe what its sprite looks like and how it should visually change when it's doing well versus struggling — this is the design decision the demo pet's sprite reactions gave you evidence for in Question 4.

Part 2 · Three Core Stats

Confirm the three attributes your pet will track. Most teams keep hunger, energy and happiness — the same three the demo pet used — because they cover physical need, activity, and emotional state without overlapping. For each stat, decide its starting value (usually the midpoint, 50) and which action(s) change it.

Part 3 · Win Condition

What does it look like when your pet is thriving? This isn't a one-off event like a lose condition — it's an ongoing state. Example: "all three stats stay above 70 for a full in-game day."

Part 4 · Lose Condition

What single event ends the game or triggers a serious consequence? This must be tied to a specific, testable threshold — not "the pet gets sad," but "hunger reaches 0." You'll turn this exact condition into pseudocode in the next section.

Your finished brief goes in the Notebook Prompt at the bottom of this page — there's a structured template waiting for you there.

Researching & Planning

State Changes & Thresholds

A threshold is a specific value that, once crossed, should trigger a reaction. You already met one in Lesson 1–2's clamping pattern — but clamping stops a number going past a limit, while a threshold reacts to a number reaching one. They often work together: hunger is clamped so it can't go below 0, and reaching exactly 0 is the threshold that ends the game.

Researching & Planning

Pseudocode for a Threshold

Before Lesson 7, every threshold in your brief gets written as NSW-format pseudocode. This is the exact plan your MakeCode blocks will follow — if the pseudocode is right, translating it into blocks later is mostly mechanical.

Worked Example — Lose Condition

BEGIN
  IF hunger ≤ 0 THEN
    SET gameState TO "gameOver"
    SHOW "Your pet needs you!"
  ENDIF
END

Notice the structure: IF a condition is true, THEN do something, ENDIF closes the block. This is the exact shape you'll use for every threshold reaction in this studio — only the condition and the actions inside change.

Worked Example — A Milder Reaction (not a lose condition)

BEGIN
  IF happiness < 20 THEN
    SET petMood TO "sad"
    SET petSprite TO sadSpriteImage
  ENDIF
END

This second example doesn't end the game — it just changes how the pet looks and feels, which is a good reminder that not every threshold needs to be severe. Some thresholds warn the player; only your one lose-condition threshold should end the game.

Researching & Planning

Guided Practice — Write Your Own

Your Turn

Using the two worked examples above as your model, write NSW pseudocode for your win condition from your brief. Start with BEGIN, use exactly one IF...THEN...ENDIF block, and decide what should happen when the condition is met — a message, a sprite change, a score increase, or all three. There's space for this in the Notebook Prompt below.

Sentence starter, if you're stuck "IF [stat] [comparison] [value] THEN SET [something] TO [new value]" Fill in the brackets using your own brief's win condition. If your win condition checks more than one stat, you can chain conditions with AND — for example, IF hunger > 70 AND energy > 70 AND happiness > 70 THEN.

CPD Grade Descriptors — Stage 5

These are the NESA Course Performance Descriptors for Stage 5 Computing Technology, used to report your achievement at the end of Year 10. Stage 4 work (Years 7–8) is pitched toward these same descriptors, adjusted for your year level. They apply across every lesson in Pet Lab — not just this one. Tap a grade to expand it.

A

Demonstrates an extensive understanding of the influence of enterprise, innovation and automation on the evolution of computing technology; skilfully applies appropriate iterative processes to produce computing solutions; develops highly effective computing solutions using computational, design and systems thinking skills; selects and applies safe, secure and ethical practices in the use of data; skilfully develops, tests and implements technically concise algorithms in a general-purpose programming language; demonstrates creativity and innovation in the design and implementation of user interfaces to create engaging user experiences; selects relevant data, media and processes to effectively communicate information in a range of contexts.

B

Demonstrates a thorough understanding of the influence of enterprise, innovation and automation on the evolution of computing technology; applies appropriate iterative processes to produce computing solutions; develops effective computing solutions using computational, design and systems thinking skills; selects and applies safe, secure and ethical practices in the use of data; develops, tests and implements functional algorithms in a general-purpose programming language; demonstrates creativity in the design and implementation of user interfaces to create engaging user experiences; selects relevant data, media and processes to communicate appropriate information in a range of contexts.

C

Demonstrates a sound understanding of the influence of enterprise, innovation and automation on the evolution of computing technology; applies iterative processes to produce computing solutions; develops sound computing solutions using computational, design and systems thinking skills; applies safe, secure and ethical practices in the use of data; develops common algorithms and implements them in a general-purpose programming language; designs and implements user interfaces to create user experiences; selects appropriate data, media and processes to communicate information in a range of contexts.

D

Demonstrates a basic understanding of the influence of enterprise and innovation on the evolution of computing technology; uses processes to produce computing solutions; develops basic computing solutions using computational or design or systems thinking skills; uses data safely and responsibly; develops basic algorithms and/or implements them in a general-purpose programming language; implements basic elements of user interface design to support user experiences; uses data to communicate basic information.

E

Identifies the evolution of, and/or innovations in, computing technology; identifies processes that may produce a computing solution; works safely with data; follows algorithms and/or partially implements them in a general-purpose programming language; identifies elements of user interfaces that contribute to user experiences; uses data to communicate information in a very limited way.
