Chapter 1

Perimeter of Quadrilaterals and Composite Figures

The Council’s Garden Bed Problem

A local council is building a new L-shaped garden bed outside the library.

The landscaper has given the plan below, but only some of the side lengths are marked.

The council needs to order edging timber for the entire outside boundary.

Can you work out the total length of edging timber needed — even though two of the side lengths aren’t marked on the plan?
I Do — Worked Examples

Watch how it’s done

Find the perimeter of this parallelogram.

This is a parallelogram, so opposite sides are equal in length.

The side lengths are 8 cm and 5 cm.

Add the first pair of equal sides (highlighted green): 8 + 8 = 16 cm.

Add the second pair of equal sides (highlighted orange): 5 + 5 = 10 cm.

Perimeter = 16 + 10 = 26 cm.

Find the perimeter of this composite figure.

The outer rectangle would be 12 cm by 10 cm, but a 4 cm by 5 cm notch has been cut from the top-right corner.

Two of the six side lengths are not directly marked.

Instead of finding those two missing sides one at a time, extend them (green dashed lines) to complete the full outer rectangle.

The extended rectangle is 12 cm wide and 10 cm tall.

Top + bottom = 12 + 12 = 24 cm.

Left + right = 10 + 10 = 20 cm.

Perimeter = 24 + 20 = 44 cm.

This is the same total you’d get by adding all six real sides one at a time — the extend method is just faster.

A community sports field has a pointed end.
Find the boundary length.

Add all five real sides of the boundary: the two long sides, the back end, and the two angled sides at the point.

P = 20 + 20 + 9 + 11 + 12
P = 72 m

The perimeter is 72 m.

Two methods, same answer — which is faster?

For a composite figure like the one below, there are two common ways to find the perimeter.

Method A — Extend

Extend the missing sides to complete the full 12 m × 10 m rectangle, then add.

12 + 10 + 12 + 10 = 44 m

Method B — Add the real sides

Work out the two missing sides (12−4=8, 10−5=5) and add all six real sides.

12 + 4 + 8 + 5 + 5 + 10 = 44 m

Both methods give the same answer — 44 m — but Method A needs far fewer calculation steps, especially if a figure has several notches.

Newman’s Word Problem

A school is building a raised garden bed shaped like a rectangle with a small rectangular corner cut out for a path.
The main rectangle is 8 m long and 5 m wide.
The corner cut out is 2 m long and 1.5 m wide.
The school wants to buy edging timber for the entire outside boundary of the garden bed.
How many metres of edging timber do they need?
