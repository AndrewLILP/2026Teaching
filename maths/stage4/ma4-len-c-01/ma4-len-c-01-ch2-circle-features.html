Chapter 2

Features of a Circle

Why Does a Wider Yidaki Sound Deeper?

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander instruments like the yidaki (didgeridoo) and clap sticks come in many different sizes.

A wider yidaki produces a deeper, lower sound than a narrow one.

To explain why, we first need the right words for exactly what we’re measuring on a circular cross-section.

A wider cross-section means a longer diameter — but what’s the exact relationship between a circle’s different features, and why does that affect the sound?
I Do — Worked Examples

Watch how it’s done

Label all 7 features of this circle, one at a time.

Here is a circle.

We’ll label its 7 features one at a time.

This is the radius — the distance from the centre to any point on the circle.

This is the diameter — a straight line through the centre, touching the circle at both ends.

This is a chord — a straight line joining two points on the circle, but not through the centre.

This is an arc — a curved part of the circle’s edge between two points.

This is a sector — the region enclosed by two radii and an arc, like a pizza slice.

This is a segment — the region enclosed by a chord and the arc it cuts off.

This is a tangent — a straight line that touches the circle at exactly one point, without crossing inside.

All 7 features are now labelled.

This circle has two straight lines and two shaded regions.

One line passes through the very centre of the circle.

The other line does not.

The green line passes through the centre — this makes it the diameter.

The slate line does not pass through the centre — this makes it a chord.

Region A is bounded by two radii and an arc, meeting at the centre point.

This makes Region A a sector.

Region B is bounded by a chord and the arc it cuts off, and does not include the centre point.

This makes Region B a segment.

Both lines below appear to just touch this yidaki cross-section.

Only one is truly a tangent.

Line 1 (green) touches the circle’s edge at exactly one point and never crosses into the interior.

This makes Line 1 a tangent.

Line 2 (terracotta) looks similar, but it actually crosses into the circle’s interior, touching the edge at two points that happen to be very close together.

This makes Line 2 a chord, not a tangent — even though it looks almost the same at a glance.

The test that matters: does the line ever cross inside the circle?

If yes, even briefly, it’s a chord.

If it only ever touches the boundary at a single point, it’s a tangent.

Newman’s Word Problem

A didgeridoo maker cuts a circular disc of wood to make the mouthpiece.
The disc has a straight cut passing through its exact centre, from one edge to the other, measuring 9 cm.
A separate straight cut is made that touches the circle’s edge at just one point, without entering the circle at all.
What is each of these two cuts called, and how are the two radii related to the diameter?

Stage 1 — Read

Read the problem carefully.
Reread it if anything is unclear.

Stage 2 — Comprehend

What is the problem actually asking you to find?

Sentence starter: "The problem is asking me to name two …"

Stage 3 — Transform

Which circle features match each description?

Sentence starter: "The cut through the centre is called a …, and the cut touching at one point is called a …"

Stage 4 — Process

Work out how the radius relates to the diameter.

Sentence starter: "Since the diameter is 9 cm, the radius is 9 ÷ 2 = …"

Stage 5 — Encode

Write your final answer in a full sentence.

The cut through the centre is the diameter (9 cm), and the cut touching at one point is the tangent.
The radius is always half the diameter, so the radius here is 9 ÷ 2 = 4.5 cm.
The diameter is 9 cm and the radius is 4.5 cm.
