Chapter 3

Circumference of a Circle

The Belt Around the Earth

Imagine a belt fitted snugly around the entire equator of the Earth.

Assume Earth is a perfect sphere with radius 6400 km.

Now imagine adding just 1 extra metre to the length of that belt, so it hangs slightly loose all the way around.

If we add just 1 metre to a belt wrapped around the entire Earth, would the gap be big enough to slide a coin under, a cat under, or a person under?
I Do — Worked Examples

Watch how it’s done

A bicycle wheel has diameter 60 cm.
Find the distance it covers in one full rotation (its circumference).

We want to find how far the wheel’s edge travels in one full turn — its circumference.

If unrolled, the circle’s edge stretches just over 3 diameters.

This ratio — circumference ÷ diameter — is always the same number for every circle, called π (pi) ≈ 3.14159.

C = π × d

C = π × 60 ≈ 188.5 cm (to 1 decimal place)

The wheel covers about 188.5 cm in one full rotation.

A circular pool has radius 3.5 m.
Find its circumference.

Since we’re given the radius, we use C = 2 × π × r.

C = 2 × π × 3.5

C ≈ 22.0 m (to 1 decimal place)

The pool’s circumference is about 22.0 m.

A wheel has circumference 220 cm and makes 50 complete rotations.

Find the total distance travelled, then work out the wheel’s radius.

Total distance = circumference × number of rotations.

Total distance = 220 × 50 = 11 000 cm = 110 m.

To find the radius, rearrange C = 2π·r to make r the subject: r = C ÷ (2π).

r = 220 ÷ (2 × π)

r ≈ 35.0 cm (to 1 decimal place)

Discover π for yourself

Three circular objects were measured, giving both their diameter and circumference.

12.6 ÷ 4 = 3.15

22.0 ÷ 7 = 3.14

31.4 ÷ 10 = 3.14

No matter the size of the circle, circumference ÷ diameter is always very close to 3.14159…

This constant is called π (pi).

Newman’s Word Problem

A circular running track has a radius of 35 m.
An athlete plans to run exactly 5 laps of the track during training.
Approximately how far will the athlete run in total?
Give your answer to the nearest metre.

Stage 1 — Read

Read the problem carefully.
Reread it if anything is unclear.

Stage 2 — Comprehend

What is the problem actually asking you to find?

Sentence starter: "The problem is asking me to find the total …"

Stage 3 — Transform

Which method will you use to solve this?

Sentence starter: "First I’ll find the circumference using …, then I’ll …"

Stage 4 — Process

Carry out the calculation.

Sentence starter: "C = 2 × π × 35 = …, then multiply by 5 laps."

Stage 5 — Encode

Write your final answer in a full sentence.

C = 2 × π × 35 ≈ 219.9 m.
Total for 5 laps = 219.9 × 5 = 1099.6 m, which rounds to 1100 m.
The athlete will run approximately 1100 m.
