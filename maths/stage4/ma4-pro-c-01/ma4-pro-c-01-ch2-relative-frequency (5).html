I Do

What is relative frequency?

Relative frequency is the observed probability from repeated trials of a chance experiment.

Relative frequency = number of times the outcome occurredtotal number of trials

I Do

Worked example: the drawing pin toss

A drawing pin is tossed 50 times. It lands point-up 32 times.

Relative frequency = 3250 = 1625

This is the observed probability, based on what actually happened in these 50 tosses.

I Do

Observed vs theoretical

Back in Chapter 1, we said a standard 6-sided die has a theoretical probability of 16 for rolling any number.

But suppose a standard 6-sided die is actually rolled 120 times, and a 6 appears 30 of those times.

Observed (relative frequency) = 30120 = 14

Theoretical probability = 16

These aren't the same — and that's completely normal. A simulation uses a random number generator to repeat a chance experiment many times. The more trials we run, the closer the relative frequency usually gets to the theoretical probability.

Every relative frequency is close to 0.167, but none is exactly equal to it — that's expected with real (or simulated) data. Number 6 came out slightly higher (0.175) and Number 1 slightly lower (0.160). With even more trials, these values would likely drift even closer to 0.167.

We Do

Resolving the Hook

So — can both Coin A and Coin B really be fair coins?

Yes, both coins could still be fair. With only 20 tosses, results like 14 heads and 6 tails happen fairly often just by chance — this is called natural variation.

We only start to suspect a coin is biased (unfairly weighted toward one outcome) when the relative frequency stays far from 0.5 even after many more trials.
