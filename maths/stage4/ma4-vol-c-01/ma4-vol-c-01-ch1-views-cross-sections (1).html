Views and Cross-Sections of Prisms

Mystery Stack 🔍

Here is the top view of a solid built from stacked rectangular blocks.
It's just a plain rectangle — that's all the information you're given.

A simple rectangle.
That seems like enough to know exactly what the solid looks like — doesn't it?

Sketch (in your head, or on paper) two completely different solids that could both give this exact same top view.

Solid A is a plain flat box.
Solid B is a stepped tower with a taller section at the back.
Both would draw the exact same rectangle if you only looked from directly above.

One view is never enough to describe a 3D solid completely — you need to look from more than one direction. That's exactly what this chapter is about.

Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip it and see the full definition.

Viewtap to flip

Definition: what a solid looks like when seen from one direction — usually top, front, side, or back.

Example: the front view of a shoebox is a rectangle.

Non-example: a photo taken from an angle showing three faces at once is not a single "view" in this sense.

Cross-sectiontap to flip

Definition: the 2D shape you'd see if you sliced straight through a solid.

Example: slicing a loaf of bread gives a cross-section shaped like the loaf's end.

Non-example: the outer surface (skin) of the solid is not a cross-section — it's a slice through the solid.

Uniformtap to flip

Definition: staying exactly the same shape and size all the way through — a uniform cross-section doesn't change no matter where you slice.

Example: a rectangular prism sliced anywhere along its length always gives the same rectangle.

Non-example: a pyramid's cross-section shrinks the closer you slice to the tip — not uniform.

Prismtap to flip

Definition: a solid with two identical, parallel end-faces connected by flat rectangular side-faces — it has a uniform cross-section along its length.

Example: a triangular prism, a rectangular prism (box), a hexagonal prism.

Non-example: a cylinder is not a prism — its curved surface means it isn't built from flat side-faces (though it does share the uniform cross-section idea).

I Do — Worked Examples

Modelling views and cross-sections

Choose a tier to see a fully worked example. Tap "Show next step" to reveal the reasoning one step at a time.

A rectangular box measures 4 cm wide, 3 cm deep, and 2 cm tall.
Let's find its top, front, and side views.

Step 1. Look straight down from above — this gives the top view.
It shows the box's width (4 cm) and depth (3 cm), drawn as a 4×3 rectangle.
Step 2. Look from directly in front — this gives the front view.
It shows the box's width (4 cm) and height (2 cm), drawn as a 4×2 rectangle.
Step 3. Look from the side — this gives the side view.
It shows the box's depth (3 cm) and height (2 cm), drawn as a 3×2 rectangle.
Step 4. Together, the three views fully describe the box.
No single view alone would have been enough — each one only captures two of the three dimensions.

A solid is formed by combining two rectangular prisms: a large base prism with a smaller prism sitting on its back edge, forming a step shape.

Step 1. The large prism forms the base — its footprint is the widest part of the solid.
Step 2. The smaller prism sits on the back edge of the base, so the top view shows two overlapping rectangles — an L-shaped outline.
Step 3. The front view shows the step clearly — a wide low section with a narrower tall section behind it.
Step 4. The side view is different again — it shows the full height of the tall section along its whole depth, since the side view "sees through" the step from that direction.
Step 5. Front, side, and top views each tell a different part of the story — none of them alone shows that this solid is actually two prisms combined.

You are only given two views of an unfamiliar solid — no 3D picture. Top view: a plain rectangle. Front view: a step shape (short section, then taller section).
Question: does this solid have a uniform cross-section?

Step 1. The top view being a plain rectangle tells us the footprint doesn't change shape when viewed from above.
Step 2. But the front view shows a step — meaning the height changes partway across the solid.
Step 3. A uniform cross-section means the same shape all the way through, when sliced parallel to the base, at any height.
Step 4. Since the height changes, slicing this solid horizontally at different heights would give different-shaped cross-sections — smaller near the top, larger lower down.
Step 5. So no — this solid does not have a uniform cross-section, even though its top view looked perfectly simple. One view was not enough to tell.

Newman's Word Problem

A shop assistant sketches only the front view of a stepped display shelf and says this is enough information to build an identical copy. Explain why the assistant is wrong, and describe what additional view(s) they would need.

Stage 1 — Read

Need a sentence starter?

The important words in this problem are ___, ___, and ___.

Identify the key terms in the problem before moving on.

Stage 2 — Comprehend

Need a sentence starter?

The assistant is claiming that ___ is enough information to ___.

What exactly is being claimed, and what are you being asked to test?

Stage 3 — Transform

Need a sentence starter?

To test this claim, I need to think about what a front view does and doesn't show — this is a ___ question, not a calculation.

Decide what kind of reasoning this problem actually needs.

Stage 4 — Process

Need a sentence starter?

A front view only shows ___ and ___ — it doesn't show ___.

Work through the reasoning: what information does a front view capture, and what does it miss?

Stage 5 — Encode

Write your final explanation, then compare it to the worked solution.

A front view only shows width and height — it says nothing about depth.
Two solids with completely different depths (a thin shelf vs. a deep one) could share the identical front view.
The assistant would also need the side view (to capture depth) and ideally the top view as well, to be certain the copy is identical in every dimension.
