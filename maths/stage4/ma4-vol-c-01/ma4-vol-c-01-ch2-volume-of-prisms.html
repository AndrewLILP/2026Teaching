Volume of Prisms

The Same Box, Twice the Size? 📦

A cube-shaped storage box has a volume of 125 cm³.
Someone doubles every dimension — width, depth, and height are all now twice as big.

Prediction time: does the volume also just double, to 250 cm³?

Doubling every dimension of a cube means multiplying each of the 3 dimensions by 2.
That's 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 times bigger overall — not 2 times.
So the new volume is 125 × 8 = 1000 cm³, not 250 cm³.

This "doubling trap" catches almost everyone the first time. By the end of this chapter you'll be able to prove exactly why it happens.

Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip it and see the full definition.

Volumetap to flip

Definition: the amount of 3D space a solid takes up, measured in cubic units.

Example: a shipping box has a volume you could fill with water, sand, or smaller boxes.

Non-example: the area of one face of the box is not its volume — area is flat (2D), volume is 3D.

Base areatap to flip

Definition: the area of the prism's uniform cross-sectional face — the shape that gets "pushed through" to form the prism.

Example: for a rectangular prism, the base area is length × width.

Non-example: the total surface area (all faces added together) is a different, larger quantity.

Cubic unittap to flip

Definition: the unit used to measure volume — a cube measuring 1 unit on every edge.

Example: cm³ (cubic centimetres), m³ (cubic metres).

Non-example: cm² measures area (flat, 2D) — it can never be used to describe volume.

Right prismtap to flip

Definition: a prism whose side faces stand exactly perpendicular to the base — it doesn't lean.

Example: a standard rectangular box or triangular prism sitting upright.

Non-example: an oblique (leaning) prism has slanted side faces — the volume formula V = A·h still needs the perpendicular height, not the slanted edge length.

I Do — Worked Examples

Modelling volume of prisms

Choose a tier to see a fully worked example. Tap "Show next step" to reveal the reasoning one step at a time.

A rectangular prism measures 6 cm long, 4 cm wide, and 3 cm tall. Find its volume.

Step 1. The familiar way: Volume = length × width × height = 6 × 4 × 3.
Step 2. Regroup this as (6 × 4) × 3 — that first bracket is the area of the base rectangle.
Step 3. Base area, A = 6 × 4 = 24 cm².
Step 4. Volume = base area × height = A × h = 24 × 3 = 72 cm³.
Step 5. This is the general formula V = A·h — and it works for every prism, not just rectangular ones. That's exactly what makes it so useful.

A triangular prism has a triangular cross-section with base 8 cm and height 5 cm. The prism itself is 10 cm long. Find its volume.

Step 1. First find the area of the triangular cross-section: A = 12 × base × height.
Step 2. A = 12 × 8 × 5 = 20 cm².
Step 3. The prism's length — how far that triangle is "pushed through" — is 10 cm.
Step 4. Volume = base area × height = A · h = 20 × 10 = 200 cm³.
Step 5. Same formula as the rectangular prism — V = A·h — just with a triangular base area this time instead of a rectangular one.

A cube-shaped storage box has a volume of 125 cm³. Find the side length, then work out what happens to the volume if every dimension is doubled.

Step 1. For a cube, all three dimensions are equal, so V = s × s × s = s³.
Step 2. We need s³ = 125. Since 5 × 5 × 5 = 125, the side length is s = 5 cm.
Step 3. Now double every dimension: the new side length is 10 cm.
Step 4. New volume = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000 cm³.
Step 5. Compare: 125 cm³ became 1000 cm³ — that's 8 times bigger, not 2 times. Doubling every dimension multiplies the volume by 2 × 2 × 2 = 8, because volume grows with all three dimensions at once.

Newman's Word Problem

A juice company sells juice in triangular-prism cartons. Each carton has a triangular base with a base of 6 cm and height 8 cm, and the carton is 15 cm long. The company wants to redesign the carton to hold exactly double the volume, without changing the triangular base at all. What should the new length of the carton be?
