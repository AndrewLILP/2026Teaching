Volume of Cylinders

Cube Tank vs Cylinder Tank 🛢️

A council is comparing two water tank designs with almost the same footprint.
Tank A is a cube: 1.5 m × 1.5 m × 1.5 m.
Tank B is a cylinder: 1.6 m diameter, 1.6 m tall.

Which tank holds more water — and by how much? Have a guess before you continue.

Tank A (cube): 1.5 × 1.5 × 1.5 = 3.375 m³.
Tank B (cylinder): radius = 0.8 m, so V = π·r²·h = π × 0.8² × 1.6 ≈ 3.217 m³.

Despite looking like a close match, the cube actually holds more — about 0.158 m³ more. A circle drawn inside a square always covers less area than the square itself, so a cylinder "squeezed" into the same footprint as a cube always comes up short. You'll be able to prove this kind of comparison yourself by the end of this chapter.

Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip it and see the full definition.

Radiustap to flip

Definition: the distance from the centre of a circle to any point on its edge.

Example: a circle with radius 5 cm is 5 cm from centre to edge, all the way around.

Non-example: the distance all the way across the circle, through the centre, is the diameter — not the radius.

Diametertap to flip

Definition: the distance straight across a circle, through the centre — always exactly double the radius.

Example: a circle with diameter 10 cm has a radius of 5 cm.

Non-example: a line from the centre to the edge only (not passing all the way through) is a radius, not a diameter.

π (pi)tap to flip

Definition: a fixed number, approximately 3.14159, equal to a circle's circumference divided by its diameter — the same for every circle, no matter the size.

Example: calculators have a π button because this number is used constantly in circle and cylinder formulas.

Non-example: rounding π to exactly 3 is a rough estimate only — it isn't the true value, and using it gives a less accurate answer.

Cylindertap to flip

Definition: a solid with two identical circular ends connected by a curved side — a uniform circular cross-section all the way through.

Example: a can of soup, a water tank, a pipe.

Non-example: a cone is not a cylinder — its circular cross-section shrinks to a point instead of staying constant.

I Do — Worked Examples

Modelling volume of cylinders

Choose a tier to see a fully worked example. Tap "Show next step" to reveal the reasoning one step at a time.

A cylinder has radius 3 cm and height 10 cm. Find its volume.

Step 1. The formula is V = π·r²·h.
Step 2. Substitute r = 3 and h = 10: V = π × 3² × 10.
Step 3. V = π × 9 × 10 = 90π.
Step 4. Using a calculator value for π: V ≈ 282.7 cm³.
Step 5. Think of it as 10 identical circular layers, each with area π×3²≈28.3 cm², stacked 1 cm thick: 28.3 × 10 ≈ 282.7 cm³ — same answer, different way of seeing it. The Explore tab digs into this idea properly.

A cylindrical tank has diameter 1.6 m and height 1.6 m. Find its volume.

Step 1. The formula needs the radius — but we're given the diameter. Halve it first: r = 1.6 ÷ 2 = 0.8 m.
Step 2. Now apply V = π·r²·h with r = 0.8 and h = 1.6.
Step 3. V = π × 0.8² × 1.6 = π × 0.64 × 1.6.
Step 4. V ≈ 3.217 m³.
Step 5. This is the exact tank from the Hook — and it's slightly less than the 3.375 m³ cube tank, confirming what you saw earlier.

A hollow cylindrical pipe has an outer radius of 6 cm, an inner radius of 4 cm, and is 20 cm long. Find the volume of material used to make the pipe.

Step 1. The pipe is a big cylinder (outer radius 6 cm) with a smaller cylinder (inner radius 4 cm) removed from the middle — the hollow part.
Step 2. Volume of material = volume of outer cylinder − volume of inner cylinder.
Step 3. Outer: V = π × 6² × 20 = π × 36 × 20 = 720π.
Step 4. Inner: V = π × 4² × 20 = π × 16 × 20 = 320π.
Step 5. Material = 720π − 320π = 400π ≈ 1256.6 cm³.

Explore — Stacking Circular Layers

Where does V = π·r²·h actually come from? Let's build a cylinder one thin layer at a time and see.

Each layer below is a circle with radius 3 cm, about 1 cm thick. Its area is π × 3² ≈ 28.3 cm².

1 layer.

Just one flat disc. Its area is ≈ 28.3 cm² — but a single layer isn't a cylinder yet, it has almost no height.

2 layers.

Stack a second identical layer on top. Height is now 2 cm. Total volume so far: 28.3 × 2 ≈ 56.5 cm³ — we're just adding one more identical layer's worth of space.

5 layers.

Height is now 5 cm. Total volume: 28.3 × 5 ≈ 141.4 cm³. Each dashed line marks where one layer ends and the next begins.

10 layers — a complete cylinder.

Height is now 10 cm, matching the Foundation worked example exactly. Total volume: 28.3 × 10 ≈ 282.7 cm³.

The generalisation.

Every layer contributes the same circular area, π·r². Stack "h" layers, each 1 unit thick, and the total volume is (area of one layer) × (number of layers) = π·r² × h.

That's exactly the formula V = π·r²·h — it's not a rule to memorise blindly, it's just "layer area × how many layers."

Newman's Word Problem

A council is comparing two water pipes for a new park irrigation system. Pipe A has a diameter of 10 cm and is 200 cm long. Pipe B has a diameter of 14 cm and is 150 cm long. The council wants the pipe that can carry the most water at any one moment. Which pipe should they choose, and how much more volume does it hold than the other?

Stage 1 — Read

Need a sentence starter?

The important numbers are ___ (Pipe A) and ___ (Pipe B), and the goal is to find ___.

Identify the key numbers for each pipe and what you're being asked to find.

Stage 2 — Comprehend

Need a sentence starter?

"Carries the most water at once" means comparing the ___ of each pipe.

What does "carry the most water at any one moment" actually mean mathematically?

Stage 3 — Transform

Need a sentence starter?

Both diameters need to be ___ before using V = π·r²·h for each pipe.

Decide what needs to happen to the given measurements before you can apply the formula.

Stage 4 — Process

Need a sentence starter?

Pipe A: r = ___, V ≈ ___. Pipe B: r = ___, V ≈ ___. Difference = ___.

Work through the calculation for both pipes, then compare.

Stage 5 — Encode

Write your final answer, then compare it to the worked solution.

Pipe A: r = 5 cm, V = π × 5² × 200 = 5000π ≈ 15708 cm³.
Pipe B: r = 7 cm, V = π × 7² × 150 = 7350π ≈ 23091 cm³.
Pipe B holds more — about 23091 − 15708 ≈ 7383 cm³ more than Pipe A.
