How long is the side of this square?

Hook

A square has an area of exactly 2 square units.
That's a simple number to write down.
But what about the length of one side?

Work through the questions below one at a time.
Reveal the next question only after you've had a go at the current one.

Step 1 — Bound it between whole numbers

Between which two whole numbers does the side length lie?
Think about which whole number squared gives you just under 2, and which gives you just over 2.

1² = 1, which is less than 2.
2² = 4, which is more than 2.
So the side length is somewhere between 1 and 2.

Step 2 — Narrow it to one decimal place

Between which two decimal numbers (to 1 decimal place) does the side length lie?

1.4² = 1.96, which is less than 2.
1.5² = 2.25, which is more than 2.
So the side length is somewhere between 1.4 and 1.5.

Step 3 — Where does it sit on a number line?

Picture a number line from 0 to 2.
Roughly where would you mark this side length?
Hold that picture in your mind — you'll build this exact point with a compass in the Construct It tab.

Step 4 — Approximate it

Using a calculator or digital tool, this side length is approximately 1.41421356...
Notice that the decimal doesn't terminate, and it doesn't seem to repeat in a pattern either.
This number has a name: it's called √2, and it's an example of an irrational number.

In this chapter you'll learn what makes a number irrational, how to round and approximate numbers sensibly, and how to tell terminating decimals apart from recurring ones.

Rounding and approximation

Suppose you need to find the volume of a cylinder with radius r and height h, using the formula V = π·r²·h.
If you then need to work out how many 250 mL cups the cylinder could fill, an exact answer written in terms of π isn't actually useful — you can't measure out “π millilitres” of anything.
In a case like this, rounding to a sensible number of decimal places is more appropriate than leaving the exact value.

We use the symbol ≈ to show that a value is an approximation rather than an exact answer.
For example, π ≈ 3.14.

The reverse is also true: sometimes an exact answer is what's needed — for example, if you're proving a general rule algebraically, rounding partway through could introduce an error into your reasoning.

Terminating and recurring decimals

Some fractions convert to decimals that stop — these are called terminating decimals.
Other fractions convert to decimals with a digit or block of digits that repeats forever — these are called recurring decimals.

We can write recurring decimals using dot notation or vinculum (bar) notation:

13 = 0.333... = 0.3̇
16 = 0.1666... = 0.16̇
0.345345345... = 0.3̄4̄5̄

We Do

Classify the pattern

Divide 1 by each of the single-digit numbers 2 through 9 on a calculator.
For each result, decide whether it's terminating or recurring.
Which denominators seem to give terminating decimals? What do those denominators have in common?

1÷2, 1÷4, 1÷5, and 1÷8 all terminate.
1÷3, 1÷6, 1÷7, and 1÷9 all recur.
The terminating ones have denominators built only from the prime factors 2 and 5 — this is a pattern worth noticing, even though you don't need to prove it yet.
I Do

Irrational numbers

A rational number can always be written in the form ab, where a and b are integers and b ≠ 0.
An irrational number cannot be written this way.

√2 and π are both irrational.
You may have used 227 as a stand-in for π in earlier calculations — but 227 is only a rational approximation of π, not its exact value.

Construct It: locating √2 exactly

Construct It

Even though √2 can't be written as a terminating or recurring decimal, it still has one exact position on the number line.
Here's how a compass can find that position precisely, using the right-angled triangle from the Hook.

Start with a right-angled triangle with both legs equal to 1 unit, sitting on the number line.
Its hypotenuse has length √2, by Pythagoras' theorem: 1² + 1² = 2, so the hypotenuse squared is 2.
Swing that hypotenuse down onto the number line with a compass, keeping the same length.
The point where the arc lands is exactly √2 — not an approximation, but the precise location of an irrational number.

This is the same idea you used in the Hook when you narrowed the side length down between 1.4 and 1.5 — except a compass construction gives you the exact point, not just a decimal estimate of it.

Reflect

Key ideas from this chapter

Rounding and the ≈ symbol let us give sensible approximations, but exact values still matter in some situations.
Terminating decimals stop; recurring decimals repeat a digit or block of digits forever.
Rational numbers can be written as a fraction of integers; irrational numbers, like √2 and π, cannot — yet they still have an exact position on the number line.
