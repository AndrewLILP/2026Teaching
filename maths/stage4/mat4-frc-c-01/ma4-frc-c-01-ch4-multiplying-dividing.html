Chapter 4: Multiplying & Dividing Fractions and Decimals

MA4-FRC-C-01 · Chapter 4 of 5

More or less?

Hook

A cookie recipe makes a full batch using 23 cup of sugar.
You only want to make half a batch.

Before calculating anything, make a guess:
Will you need more or less than 13 cup of sugar?

Half of 23 is exactly 13 — not more, not less.
12 × 23 = 26 = 13

If that felt slightly surprising, you're not alone — multiplying by a fraction less than 1 can feel like it should "do more" than it actually does.
In this chapter, you'll build a solid feel for exactly what multiplying and dividing by fractions and decimals does to a quantity, whether that quantity grows or shrinks, and how to calculate it precisely either way.

Learn

I Do

The magnitude effect

10 × 14 = 2.5, which is smaller than 10.
This happens because multiplying by 14 finds one quarter of the original amount — and a quarter of something is always smaller than the whole thing.

This isn't a special rule just for 14 — it's true for any number strictly between 0 and 1.
Multiplying by a number less than 1 always produces a result smaller than what you started with.
Multiplying by a number greater than 1 always produces a result larger than what you started with.

We Do

Predict, then check

Without calculating, will multiplying 340 by 0.87 give a result bigger or smaller than 340?
Make your prediction, then check it with a calculation.

0.87 is less than 1, so the result will be smaller than 340.
340 × 0.87 = 295.8, which is indeed smaller than 340 — the prediction holds.

Multiplying fractions

13 × 45 = 415, which we can see using an area model.

One row (a third of the grid) is shaded to represent 13.
Four columns (four fifths of the grid) are shaded to represent 45.
Where the shading overlaps — 4 cells out of 15 — is the product, 415.

We Do

Estimate, then calculate exactly

Before calculating exactly, estimate 325 × 247 by rounding each mixed number to the nearest whole number first.
Then calculate the exact answer and compare.

Estimate: 325 rounds to about 3, and 247 rounds to about 2 or 3, so the estimate is roughly 8 to 9.
Exact: 325 = 175 and 247 = 187.
175 × 187 = 30635 = 82635 ≈ 8.74.
That matches the estimate — a good sign the exact calculation is reasonable.
I Do

Dividing decimals

0.6 ÷ 0.2 asks: how many groups of 0.2 fit into 0.6?

Each pair of segments above is one group of 0.2.
There are exactly 3 groups of 0.2 in 0.6, so 0.6 ÷ 0.2 = 3.

We Do

Dividing fractions

Estimate first: is 23 ÷ 119 likely to be bigger or smaller than 1?
Since 23 is less than the divisor 119, the answer should be less than 1.

To divide by a fraction, multiply by its reciprocal instead.

119 = 109, so dividing by it means multiplying by its reciprocal, 910.
23 ÷ 109 = 23 × 910 = 1830 = 35.
35 = 0.6, which is indeed less than 1, matching the estimate.

Reflect

Key ideas from this chapter

Multiplying by a number less than 1 always shrinks the original amount; multiplying by a number greater than 1 always grows it.
Multiplying fractions means multiplying numerators together and denominators together — an area model shows why this works.
Dividing by a fraction is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal.
Estimating before calculating exactly is a reliable way to check whether your final answer is reasonable.
