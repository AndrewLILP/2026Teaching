Chapter 5: Percentages in Context

MA4-FRC-C-01 · Chapter 5 of 5

Up 20%, then down 20% — back to normal?

Hook

A jacket is priced at $100.
During a sale, the price is discounted by 20%.
When the sale ends, the store increases the sale price by 20% to bring it back.

Before calculating anything, make a prediction:
Is the jacket now back to exactly $100?

Not quite — it's actually $96, not $100.

Discount: $100 × 0.80 = $80
Increase: $80 × 1.20 = $96

The 20% decrease and the 20% increase are calculated on different starting amounts — $100 the first time, but only $80 the second time.
That's why the two changes don't cancel out.

This chapter is about calculating percentage increases, decreases, and comparisons confidently — including cases like this one, where intuition can lead you astray.

Learn

I Do

One quantity as a percentage of another

A runner completes 750 m of a 2 km race.
To express this as a percentage, both quantities must be in the same units first.

2 km = 2000 m
750 ÷ 2000 × 100 = 37.5%

Forgetting to convert units before dividing is one of the most common mistakes with this type of question — always check both quantities are measured the same way first.

We Do

Percentage decrease

A laptop was originally selling for $2780, and its price was reduced by 28% during an online shopping event.
What is the final price?

A 28% decrease means the final price is 100% − 28% = 72% of the original.
$2780 × 0.72 = $2001.60
I Do

The multiplier method

Multiplying by 1.05 increases a number by 5%, because 1.05 = 100% + 5%.
Multiplying by 0.87 decreases a number by 13%, because 0.87 = 100% − 13%.

This multiplier method is usually faster than calculating the percentage separately and then adding or subtracting it.

We Do

Why the Hook doesn't return to $100

Using the multiplier method, explain algebraically why increasing an amount by 20% and then decreasing the result by 20% never returns to the original amount, for any starting price.

Increasing by 20% multiplies by 1.20.
Decreasing by 20% multiplies by 0.80.
Combined, the overall multiplier is 1.20 × 0.80 = 0.96, not 1.
Since 0.96 is less than 1, the final amount is always 96% of the original — a 4% loss, no matter what the starting price is.
I Do

GST

GST (Goods and Services Tax) adds 10% to the pre-tax price of most goods and services in Australia.

An electrical job costs $720 before GST.
$720 × 1.10 = $792

We Do

Profit and loss as a percentage

A market stallholder buys handmade candles for $12 each and sells them for $15 each.
What is the profit as a percentage of the cost price?

Profit = $15 − $12 = $3.
Profit as a percentage of the cost price: $3 ÷ $12 × 100 = 25%.
Notice this is different from profit as a percentage of the selling price: $3 ÷ $15 × 100 = 20%.
Always check which base amount — cost price or selling price — the question is asking for.

Question 7 · Problem Solving (Newman's Analysis)

A hardware store charges $650 for a tool before GST.
GST adds 10% to the price.
A trade discount of 15% is then applied to the GST-inclusive price for account holders.
What is the final price for an account holder?

Stage 2 — Comprehend: What is the problem asking you to find?
Stage 3 — Transform: What will you need to do, and in what order?
Stage 4 — Process: What is the price after 10% GST is added to $650?
Stage 5 — Encode: Apply the 15% trade discount to $715. What is the final price?
Price after GST: $650 × 1.10 = $715
Price after trade discount: $715 × 0.85 = $607.75
The final price for an account holder is $607.75.

Reflect

Key ideas from this chapter

Comparing two quantities as a percentage requires matching units first.
The multiplier method (like 1.05 or 0.87) is usually the fastest way to calculate a percentage increase or decrease.
A percentage increase followed by the same percentage decrease does not return to the original amount, because the second change is calculated on a different starting value.
Profit and loss percentages depend on which base amount — cost price or selling price — the question is asking about.
