MA4-DAT-C-01 · Data classification and visualisation · Year 8

Telling a story with data:
statistical infographics

Using the 2021 Census to show how Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander populations have changed.

Lesson overview · 60 minutes

Timing: Opening 3 · Hook 5 · Revision 8 · Graph Swap 10 · Justify 3 · I Do 7 · We Do 5 · You Do 14 · Exit 5.

You need: printed worksheet (5 pages, one per student), mini whiteboards, rulers, coloured pencils. Your own whiteboard beside the TV for modelling.

Keys: → or Space = next step/slide · ← = back · N = notes · F = fullscreen.

Today

What we’re learning

Learning intention

We are learning to represent a dataset using a statistical infographic and justify the graph we chose.

Success criteria — I can…

  • match a dataset to the graph type that suits it best
  • explain why another graph type would be a poor choice
  • draw an accurate graph with a title, labelled axes, a scale and a source
  • write a justification using “because…” and “…would be less suitable because…”

1 min

Reveal each criterion. Students will tick these off on page 5 of the worksheet at the end.

Hook · 10-second challenge

Look closely. What will you remember?

On your mini whiteboard

Write down every fact you can remember.
Which facts stuck?
Why those ones?
Infographics use big numbers, icons and simple graphs so information is easier to remember.

Hook · 5 min

Show for about 10 seconds, then press Hide it. Students write everything they recall (1 min).

Take answers: most will recall the big number, “1 in 10” and the trend arrow, rarely exact figures. That is the point — visual design aids retention and recall.

Press Show again to check answers.

Key idea

What makes a statistical infographic?

Statistical = it is built from real data, accurately graphed.
Not just decoration.

Every infographic needs

  • a clear title that tells the story
  • one or two big numbers
  • graphs chosen to suit each dataset
  • short captions
  • the source of the data

2 min

Point to each labelled part. Emphasise “statistical”: every graph must be accurate — correct scale, starting at zero, labelled.

Today’s worksheet asks students to make five panels that sit together as one infographic.

Revision · 3 min

Reveal row by row. Before each reveal, ask: “What graph would you use for change over time?” etc.

Graph Swap · 10 min — the core of the lesson

For each dataset, show a poor choice first, ask “What’s wrong?”, then switch to the best choice.

Suggested run: Census count → sector graph (✗ years aren’t parts of a whole) → line graph (✓).
By state → line graph (✗ states have no order) → sector (8 slices, hard to read) → column (✓).
How people identify → line (✗) → sector / divided bar (✓).

Note: the Census count is people who identify as Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander. ABS analysis found more than half of the 2016–2021 increase came from changes in identification, not only births. Good discussion for why data needs context.

Writing a justification

“Because…” is where the marks are

I chose a line graph
because the data shows change over time
and I want my reader to see how fast the count grew.
A sector graph would be less suitable because the years are not parts of one whole.
Useful words: categorical · numerical · categories · parts of a whole · adds to 100% · trend · over time · compare · proportion · in order

3 min

Read the frame aloud. The second sentence (what would be less suitable) shows deeper understanding — insist on it.

We Do · 5 min

Students vote on mini whiteboards with a reason. Click the winning choice, then try “Line” to show why it fails.

Sector and divided bar are both excellent; the worksheet gives a 100-tick circle and a 100-square bar so students choose one.

Angle check for extension students: 1% = 3.6°, so 91.4% ≈ 329°, 4.2% ≈ 15°, 4.4% ≈ 16°.

You Do · 14 min

Circulate. Common issues: scale not starting at zero; uneven scale steps; no source; justification that only names the graph without a “because”.

Panel 3: column graph sorted tallest to shortest is the strongest choice. Suggested scale 0–300,000 in steps of 50,000 (the grid has 12 squares up, so 25,000 per square).

Panel 4: 10 × 10 icon grid — shade 9 squares and half of a 10th.

Panel 5: side-by-side columns; ask “What happened to the share of older people?”

Hold sample answers back until most students have committed to a choice.

Peer check · 2 min

The answer is self-identified.
Counts grow through births and because more people choose to identify.

Reference

State counts sum to 812,497; the remaining 231 people lived in Other Territories (e.g. Jervis Bay), which is why the 2021 total is 812,728.

Age percentages for 2021 add to 100.1% because of rounding — a good “spot the rounding” question for fast finishers.
